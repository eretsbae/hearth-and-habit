Check it monthly, change it when it looks dirty, and don't go past 3 months on a 1-inch filter. Most systems can run MERV 13 if you change it often.

The monthly check and 3-month limit come from ENERGY STAR. The MERV 13 point comes from the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA). There is a catch, though. The number that decides whether a filter strains your blower is not the MERV rating. It's the airflow resistance, which some labels print right next to it.

Below you'll find an interval table by filter type, a worked example that shows how to read that resistance number, and the installation details that can quietly undo a good filter.

## The schedule: check monthly, then let your house set the pace

ENERGY STAR's advice is to check the filter every month, especially in the heavy-use months of winter and summer. If it looks dirty after a month, change it. If it doesn't, change it at least every 3 months anyway.

The EPA says to follow the filter manufacturer's recommendation, which is typically every 60 to 90 days. If the filter looks heavily soiled every time you pull it, change it more often.

Thicker filters run on a longer clock. Carrier, the HVAC manufacturer, says a 4-inch filter typically lasts around six months and some high-capacity filters last up to a year. It also notes that a 1-inch filter may need changing about monthly.

### Interval decision table

| Filter type | Starting interval | Shorten it if… |
|---|---|---|
| 1-inch fiberglass | 60–90 days is the typical manufacturer norm (EPA); never more than 3 months (ENERGY STAR); may need monthly (Carrier) | Any trigger in the list below applies, or it looks dirty at the monthly check |
| 1-inch pleated | Same clock as above: 60–90 days typical, 3-month maximum | Same triggers |
| 2-inch pleated | The sources here don't publish a separate interval, so start at ENERGY STAR's 3-month maximum and let monthly checks guide you | Same triggers |
| 4–5-inch media | About 6 months, some up to a year (Carrier). Follow the interval the filter maker gives. | Same triggers. Deep filters still get the monthly look. |
| **Any filter: replace now** | Collapsed or bent pleats, banging when the system starts, damp or moldy, or clogged (PNNL) | No waiting for the schedule |

### What "change it sooner" means in practice

Researchers at the Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL), writing guidance for the Department of Energy's Building America program, tell contractors to advise replacement every three months *or more often* when:

- the heating or cooling equipment runs six to twelve months a year
- four or more people live in the home
- there are pets
- someone smokes indoors
- someone in the home has allergies
- there's construction going on inside or outside the house

None of these sources says how much each factor shortens the interval. That's why the monthly check matters. Write the date on the frame when you install a filter, note how it looks at each check, and adjust next time.

## Which MERV rating to buy

MERV stands for Minimum Efficiency Reporting Value. It's a rating from a standardized test method set by ASHRAE, the main US heating and cooling engineering society. The test measures how well a filter catches particles from 0.3 to 10 micrometers (µm). A micrometer is a millionth of a meter, far smaller than you can see.

Here's what the EPA says each level must capture:

| MERV | 0.3–1.0 µm (smallest) | 1.0–3.0 µm | 3.0–10.0 µm (largest) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1–4 | not rated | not rated | under 20% |
| 8 | not rated | at least 20% | at least 70% |
| 11 | at least 20% | higher than MERV 8 | higher than MERV 8 |
| 13 | at least 50% | at least 85% | at least 90% |
| 16 | at least 95% | at least 95% | at least 95% |

Program minimums give you a floor. PNNL notes that ENERGY STAR-certified new homes require MERV 6 or higher, while DOE's Zero Energy Ready Home and EPA's Indoor airPLUS programs require MERV 8 or higher. PNNL also recommends MERV 13 or higher for virus control.

The EPA's advice for anyone upgrading is to use at least MERV 13, or as high as your system's fan and filter slot can accommodate. It adds that most furnaces and HVAC systems can take a MERV 13 filter without equipment problems, *provided the filter is replaced frequently*. That condition matters, and the next section explains why.

You may have heard that a furnace filter is only there to protect the equipment. PNNL lists three purposes, and occupant respiratory health comes first, especially during wildfire smoke. Protecting the HVAC equipment is second. Keeping dust off your furniture and floors is third.

## The number that matters more than MERV: pressure drop

Every filter resists airflow a little. That resistance is called **pressure drop**. It's measured in inches of water column (in. WC), a tiny unit of air pressure.

Your blower has a limited pressure "budget" to spend on the filter, the cooling coil, and all the ductwork together. The total resistance across the whole system is called total static pressure.

PNNL's guidance puts the budget this way:

- Most residential systems run efficiently when total pressure drop across the system is no higher than 0.4 to 0.5 in. WC.
- A standard PSC blower motor (permanent split capacitor, the traditional fixed-speed type) moves its required airflow at about 0.5 in. WC.
- ECM motors (electronically commutated, the variable-speed type) are most efficient between 0.3 and 0.5 in. WC.
- The filter typically accounts for 20% to 50% of the whole system's pressure drop.

PNNL also found that a higher MERV rating does not necessarily mean higher pressure drop. Filter depth and pleating matter more. Deeper or more numerous pleats give air more surface to pass through, and 4-inch-deep pleats have twice the surface area of 2-inch pleats.

### Worked example: how much of the budget does your filter use?

The formula is: **filter's rated pressure drop ÷ 0.5 = share of a PSC blower's budget.** Using PNNL's published examples:

| Filter (from PNNL's examples) | Rated pressure drop | Math | Share of 0.5 in. WC budget |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1-inch MERV 7, at 300 FPM | 0.20 in. WC | 0.20 ÷ 0.5 | 40% |
| 2-inch MERV 7, at 300 FPM | 0.13 in. WC | 0.13 ÷ 0.5 | 26% |
| 4-inch MERV 7, at 300 FPM | 0.12 in. WC | 0.12 ÷ 0.5 | 24% |
| Any filter rated 0.25 in. WC | 0.25 in. WC | 0.25 ÷ 0.5 | 50% |
| 1-inch MERV 13 (example label), at 410 CFM | 0.08 in. WC | 0.08 ÷ 0.5 | 16% |

![Bar chart of how much of a 0.5 in. WC blower pressure budget four example furnace filters use, from 16% to 50%](/images/furnace-filter-basics-diagram.svg)

The three MERV 7 filters come from one manufacturer and differ only in depth. Going from 1 inch to 4 inches nearly halves the share of the budget the filter uses.

The 1-inch MERV 13 example uses even less. That's not a perfectly fair comparison, because it was tested at a different airflow. It still shows that the MERV number alone won't tell you how hard a filter is to breathe through.

Airflow changes the answer. FPM (feet per minute) is how fast air hits the filter's face, and CFM (cubic feet per minute) is how much air moves through it.

PNNL cites a 4-inch MERV 12 rated 0.2 in. WC at 300 FPM but 0.35 at 500 FPM. That's 40% of the budget at the lower speed and 70% at the higher one. PNNL treats 500 FPM as the maximum desired face speed for homes, with 200–400 FPM preferred.

The MERV 13 label's 0.08 figure is measured at 410 CFM, and that filter is rated for up to 1,390 CFM. At higher airflow, its pressure drop will be higher. When a label lists several airflow rates, read the row closest to your system's airflow. An HVAC tech can tell you what that is.

**When two filters share a MERV rating, the one with the lower pressure drop at your system's airflow is the easier one for your blower to push through.**

### How to get that number

Since April 1, 2019, California has required HVAC filter labels to show three things:

- the MERV rating
- capture efficiency in three particle-size ranges
- pressure drop at up to five airflow rates

PNNL's example 1-inch MERV 13 label shows 62% efficiency in the smallest size range, 0.08 in. WC at 410 CFM, and a maximum rated airflow of 1,390 CFM.

Wherever you live, look for a pressure-drop figure on the package or the maker's website. PNNL advises avoiding manufacturers that won't provide static pressure figures.

Compare the new filter's pressure drop with the old filter's at a similar airflow. If the new one is higher, it uses more of your budget.

For a definite answer, have a technician measure your system's total static pressure and compare it with the 0.4–0.5 in. WC guideline. It's a quick test during a routine service visit. ### What too much resistance does, and why you may not notice

PNNL describes two different failure modes, depending on the blower motor:

- **PSC (fixed-speed) motors** can't adjust. As resistance rises, airflow "falls off" and the system runs longer to reach your thermostat setting. You may notice weaker airflow or a room that never quite gets comfortable. If that sounds familiar, see [why one room stays colder than the rest](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/09/why-is-one-room-in-my-house-always.html).
- **ECM (variable-speed) motors** ramp up speed and power to hold airflow steady. Airflow can look normal while the motor works harder and harder, and PNNL says it can do so "until it burns itself out." Burnout is more likely with an ECM than a PSC motor.

On a variable-speed system, weak airflow is not a reliable warning sign. Your furnace manual or a tech can tell you which motor type you have.

PNNL also lists other damage from excessive pressure drop. It can overheat a furnace, freeze the cooling coil, and burn out the blower motor. Clogging and filter collapse are more likely with higher-MERV filters, which is why the EPA's MERV 13 advice comes with "replaced frequently" attached. A high-MERV filter left in too long is where the risk sits.

### Does MERV 13 in a 1-inch slot work?

It can. A 1-inch filter has less pleat area than a deeper one, so check its label instead of assuming.

PNNL warns against installing a filter with a higher MERV than the system was designed for. The EPA says most systems handle MERV 13. These positions fit together once you think in terms of pressure drop.

If a 1-inch MERV 13 rates low at your airflow and you change it on schedule, you're within both pieces of advice. If you want deeper filtration than the slot allows, the EPA notes a technician can modify the system to accept a higher-efficiency filter.

None of these sources ties filter choice to warranty terms. Check your furnace manual for the filter type and size it specifies.

## Installing the filter so it actually filters

1. **Turn the system off at the thermostat** before pulling the filter.
2. **Find the slot.** It's usually where the return duct (the duct carrying air back from your rooms) meets the furnace, or behind a large return-air grille in a wall or ceiling. If the furnace sits in a hard-to-reach attic or crawlspace, PNNL recommends a filter in each return grille instead. For attic units, it recommends permanent stairs and walkways rather than balancing on ceiling joists.
3. **Match the size printed on the old frame.** The EPA notes filters commonly come 1 to 5 inches thick. A filter should fit snugly with no air sneaking around the edges, and you should never bend or crush one to make it fit.
4. **Remove the plastic wrap, but keep the cardboard frame** (PNNL).
5. **Point the arrow toward the furnace or air handler**, in the direction air flows. On a horizontal or downflow unit, trace where the return duct enters: the arrow points away from that duct and into the equipment. Carrier warns that a backward filter puts its less porous side into the airflow and can restrict airflow quickly.
6. **Close and seal the access cover.** PNNL says the panel should have a gasket and can be sealed with duct tape or magnetic tape. A missing or loose cover lets the blower pull in unconditioned or unwanted air, which is a particular concern when the furnace is in a garage.
7. **Write the date on the frame**, then turn the system back on.

## When the filter gets dirty fast

If a fresh filter turns gray within weeks, something besides normal household dust is loading it. An unsealed access cover pulling in garage or attic air is one cause PNNL flags. Construction work and a full household are others.

## Is a fresh filter worth the money?

The US Department of Energy says routinely replacing or cleaning air filters can lower an air conditioner's energy use by 5–15%. A clogged filter restricts airflow and cuts both efficiency and cooling.

To estimate your own savings, multiply the cooling portion of your monthly bill by 0.05 and by 0.15. As a hypothetical, on a $150 cooling month:

- $150 × 0.05 = $7.50
- $150 × 0.15 = $22.50

Carrier puts typical filter replacement at $10–$50. Fiberglass sits at the low end and high-efficiency pleated filters at the high end. A single filter often lasts more than one month, so in a heavy cooling season a fresh filter can come close to paying for itself.

The DOE figure covers cooling. These sources don't put a number on heating savings.

## FAQ

### Is it OK to run the system without a filter for a day or two?

None of these sources gives a safe filter-free duration. PNNL lists keeping dust off the blower and coil as one of a filter's core jobs, and an open slot can also pull in unwanted air.

If the old filter is intact, leave it in until the replacement arrives. If it has collapsed or is damp or moldy, PNNL says it needs to come out now. In that case, get a replacement the same day or keep the system off if the weather allows.

### Are washable or permanent filters better?

PNNL describes washable filters as usually electrostatic aluminum mesh or foam. They need cleaning every 1–3 months and must air-dry completely before going back in, because a damp filter can harbor mold.

Judge one the way you'd judge a disposable: by its MERV rating and its pressure drop, if the maker publishes them.

### What about FPR or MPR ratings on store-brand filters?

The EPA notes that FPR and MPR are proprietary rating systems created by individual retailers and manufacturers. MERV is based on a national consensus standard. To compare filters across brands, look for the MERV rating.

### Why doesn't my tape measure match the size printed on the filter?

The printed dimensions are a rounded "nominal" size, so the filter itself often measures a bit smaller. Buy by the size printed on the old filter or listed in your furnace manual. Whatever you buy should slide in snugly with no gaps and no bending, which is the EPA's fit test.

### Will a high-MERV filter void my furnace warranty?

None of the sources here connects filter choice to warranty terms. Your furnace manual lists the filter type and size the manufacturer specifies, so check there before upgrading.
