The way to cut your electric bill without buying anything is to change how you use what you already own — set the thermostat back while you sleep or are away, wash in cold water, dry smarter, and shut down phantom loads — and together these habits typically trim roughly 5–10% off the bill, depending on your climate and starting point. Most advice starts with spending money: a smart thermostat, new windows, a heat pump. All fine ideas — eventually. Capture the free savings first.

They're ranked roughly by impact, so start at the top.

![Six free habits that cut the electric bill: 7–10°F setback, cold washes, smarter drying, phantom loads, off-peak full loads, air-dry](/images/cut-electric-bill-no-cost-habits-diagram.svg)

## 1. Move your thermostat a few degrees

Heating and cooling are typically the largest share of a home's energy use — often somewhere around 40–50% — which makes the thermostat the highest-leverage dial in your house.

The habit: **in summer, set it a few degrees warmer when you're asleep or away; in winter, a few degrees cooler.** The U.S. Department of Energy's rule of thumb is that turning the thermostat back 7–10°F for 8 hours a day can save up to about 10% a year on heating and cooling. You don't need a smart thermostat to do this — a $0 habit or your existing programmable thermostat's schedule button does the job. For season-by-season targets, see [the right thermostat settings for every season](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/07/the-right-thermostat-settings-for-every.html).

Comfort tip: change it gradually. One degree per week barely registers, and ceiling fans (which you already own) let you feel comfortable a few degrees warmer in summer.

## 2. Wash clothes in cold water

Roughly 90% of the energy a washing machine uses goes to heating water. Modern detergents are formulated to work in cold water, and for everyday laundry the cleaning difference is negligible. Save hot washes for the rare germy load (sickness, cloth diapers). This one is a single settings change that pays every load, forever.

One catch: the savings land on your *electric* bill only if you have an electric water heater. With a gas water heater, cold-water washing trims the gas bill instead — still worth doing, just on a different line.

## 3. Stop the dryer from double-drying

The dryer is one of the hungriest appliances in the house. Three free upgrades to how you already use it:

- **Clean the lint screen every single load** — restricted airflow means longer cycles
- **Don't overstuff** — clothes need tumbling room to dry efficiently
- **Use the moisture sensor setting** instead of timed dry, so the machine stops when clothes are dry rather than when the timer says so

And in warm months, the clothesline or a drying rack is the ultimate zero-cost dryer.

## 4. Hunt down phantom loads

Electronics sip power around the clock even when "off" — TVs, game consoles, cable boxes, chargers, coffee makers with clocks. Individually small, collectively real: standby power is commonly estimated at roughly 5–10% of residential electricity use. Our breakdown of [how much phantom power really costs](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/07/phantom-power-how-much-your-idle.html) shows typical standby watts and yearly dollars by device.

The zero-cost version: **unplug what you rarely use** (the guest-room TV, the second printer) and group frequently used clusters onto power strips you already own, flipping one switch at night. Game consoles deserve special attention — check their settings for an energy-saver or "shut down instead of rest" mode.

## 5. Run full loads, and run them at night if you have time-of-use rates

Dishwashers and washing machines use nearly the same energy whether half full or full — so full loads mean fewer cycles. Then check your utility bill (or website) for **time-of-use pricing**. Many North American utilities now charge less at night and on weekends. If yours does, shifting the dishwasher and laundry to off-peak hours saves money with zero change to how much electricity you use. Most dishwashers even have a delay-start button for exactly this.

## 6. Use the dishwasher's air-dry setting

Heated dry is a sizable chunk of a dishwasher cycle's energy. Switch to air-dry (or just crack the door open after the wash ends) — we ran the numbers on [what heated dry actually costs per load](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/09/air-dry-or-heated-dry-which-one.html). Dishes dry, electrons stay home.

## 7. Let the sun work for you — in both directions

In summer, close blinds and curtains on the sunny side of the house during the day; solar gain through windows makes your AC work noticeably harder. In winter, do the reverse — open them wide on sunny days, close them at dusk to hold the warmth in. It's free passive climate control, and in sunny climates the difference is bigger than most people expect.

## 8. Cook without heating the whole kitchen

The oven is the least efficient way to heat food, especially in summer when its heat becomes your AC's problem. The microwave, toaster oven, slow cooker, or stovetop typically use a fraction of the energy for small and medium jobs. Also skip preheating for dishes that don't need it and resist opening the oven door to peek — each peek can drop the oven temperature by 25°F or more.

## 9. Turn things off — but know what matters

Lights are the classic advice, and yes, turn them off when leaving a room (with LEDs the savings are modest, but it's free). The bigger "off" wins are the ones people forget:

- **Bathroom and kitchen exhaust fans** left running for hours — in summer and winter they're actively pumping your conditioned air outdoors
- **A second fridge or freezer in the garage** that's keeping six items cold — consolidate and unplug it; an old spare fridge is frequently among the largest single loads in the house
- **Ceiling fans in empty rooms** — fans cool people, not rooms

## How to tell it's working

Don't judge by the bill total alone — rates and seasons move it around. Compare the **kWh (kilowatt-hours)** line on your bill against the same month last year; most utilities print this comparison right on the statement. Pick two or three habits, run them for a full billing cycle, and check the kWh delta.

None of these habits require sacrifice so much as attention — the electricity you're paying for is mostly doing what you told it to. Tell it something cheaper.

## FAQ

### How much can these habits realistically save?

It depends heavily on climate, home size, and where your habits start. A household that adopts the thermostat setback, cold-water washing (with an electric water heater), and phantom-load control typically lands somewhere around 5–10% — on a $150 monthly bill, roughly $7.50–$15 a month. Not life-changing, but it's recurring money for zero dollars invested.

### Isn't it cheaper to keep the house at one temperature than to reheat it?

This is the most persistent myth in home energy. For conventional systems, the answer is no — the energy saved while the house sits at the setback temperature outweighs the recovery cost. The one caveat: with a heat pump that has electric resistance backup, a big morning recovery in deep cold can kick on the expensive backup strips, so modest setbacks are safer there.

### What should I buy first, once I've done the free stuff?

When you're ready to spend: LED bulbs for any remaining incandescents, caulk and weatherstripping for drafty windows and doors (our [caulking guide](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/07/caulking-101-where-to-caulk-what-to-buy.html) shows where the gaps usually hide), and only then bigger-ticket items.
