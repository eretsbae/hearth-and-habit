Put produce where its temperature limit and its ethylene habits say it belongs: cold-sensitive crops on the counter or in the pantry, fruit in one crisper drawer, and vegetables in the other. Below is the map, plus the temperature lines behind it.

The University of California, Davis postharvest researchers put it plainly in their home storage guide. Home refrigerators usually run 38 to 42°F, and that is cold enough to damage many fruits and vegetables or keep them from ripening properly. So the question for each item is whether 38 to 42°F is below its damage line.

## Your fridge vs. each crop's damage line

"Chilling injury" means damage from cold that is well above freezing. It shows up as poor flavor, pitting, mushy spots, or produce that never ripens. Each crop has its own threshold. Here's how they compare with a fridge running 38 to 42°F:

| Crop | Where damage starts | Fridge (38–42°F) is… | Short fridge window allowed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Green (unripe) tomatoes | Below 55°F (Purdue Extension) | Well below the line | None. Ripen at room temperature |
| Red (ripe) tomatoes | Can be held at 50°F, with some loss of flavor and aroma (Purdue) | Below that | UC Davis says even red tomatoes lose flavor in the fridge |
| Peppers | Below 45°F (Purdue) | Below the line | 1–3 days if used soon after (UC Davis) |
| Cucumbers | Below 50°F for more than 2–3 days (Purdue) | Below the line | 1–3 days if used soon after (UC Davis) |
| Eggplant | UC Davis lists it as room-temperature | — | 1–3 days if used soon after (UC Davis) |
| Sweet potatoes | Below 55°F: off-flavor, discolored flesh (Iowa State) | Below the line | None. UC Davis adds they cook up with a hard core |
| Winter squash | Stored at 50–55°F (Iowa State) | Below the range | None |
| Potatoes | Below 40°F: sugary, sweet taste (Iowa State) | Straddles the line | None. See the potato section below |
| Bananas | UC Davis: black skin, no added sweetness | Damaging | None |
| Watermelon | UC Davis: loses flavor and deep red color after more than 3 days | Fine briefly | Up to 3 days |

![Bar chart comparing the cold-damage line for tomatoes, sweet potatoes, cucumbers, peppers and potatoes with a home fridge's 38 to 42°F range](/images/how-to-store-produce-fridge-map-diagram.svg)

The pattern: tomatoes, peppers, cucumbers, and sweet potatoes all have damage lines above fridge temperature, and winter squash's storage range sits above it too. Eggplant is on UC Davis's room-temperature list. Leafy greens, carrots, beets, and the cabbage family are on UC Davis's refrigerate list, which is why they belong in the crisper.

## The full storage map

These lists come from UC Davis's *Storing Fresh Fruits and Vegetables for Better Taste*, with the crisper split from Penn State's College of Agricultural Sciences.

| Where | What goes there |
|---|---|
| **Counter only** (out of direct sun) | Bananas, citrus, mangoes, cantaloupe and other muskmelons, papayas, persimmons, pineapple, plantains, pomegranates, watermelon, tomatoes, cucumbers, eggplant, peppers, basil (stems in water), ginger, jicama, pumpkins, winter squash |
| **Ventilated pantry, dark** | Potatoes, sweet potatoes, dry onions, garlic |
| **Counter until ripe, then fridge** | Avocados, kiwifruit, nectarines, peaches, pears, plums, plumcots |
| **Fruit drawer** | Apples you'll keep longer than a week, apricots, Asian pears, berries, cherries, figs, grapes, ripened fruit from the row above |
| **Vegetable drawer** | Leafy greens, lettuce, spinach, broccoli, Brussels sprouts, cabbage, cauliflower, carrots, beets, radishes, celery, asparagus, green beans, peas, leeks, green onions, mushrooms, summer squash, sweet corn, herbs except basil |
| **Fridge, any shelf** | Anything cut, peeled, or prepared |

Two items cause most of the arguments:

- **Apples.** UC Davis keeps them on the counter if you'll eat them within 7 days and in the fridge if longer. Penn State says to refrigerate apples unless they're underripe, because cold makes them less sensitive to ethylene. Either way, a bag you won't finish within a week belongs in the fruit drawer.
- **Peppers, carrots, and strawberries.** Samsung's crisper guide puts all three on the high-humidity setting. UC Davis, though, lists peppers as room-temperature produce with a 1–3 day fridge window. Carrots are no conflict: Penn State lists them as ethylene-sensitive vegetables, so they belong in the vegetable drawer anyway. Strawberries are fruit, so if they share the high-humidity drawer, keep them away from heavy ethylene producers.

## Why fruit and vegetables get separate drawers

Ethylene is a gas that ripening fruit releases. It speeds ripening and aging in anything nearby that's sensitive to it. Penn State lists the heavy producers as most tree fruits, especially apples and pears, plus apricots, avocados, cantaloupes, nectarines, papayas, and peaches. Bananas, peppers, and tomatoes give off ethylene only once fully ripe.

The damage is specific, which helps you spot it. According to Penn State:

- Carrots and parsnips turn bitter.
- Greens such as lettuce, spinach, kale, and collards, along with broccoli, cabbage, cauliflower, and celery, turn yellow and go limp.
- Cucumbers develop soft, mushy spots.
- Asparagus turns tough and woody.
- Iceberg lettuce gets rust-colored spots.
- Apples themselves turn mealy with long exposure.

UC Davis's fix is one drawer for fruit and one for vegetables. Penn State adds that the sensitive vegetables can go in paper bags rolled shut, with the fruit bagged separately in the other drawer.

If your greens wilt within days even in the right drawer, our guide on [why greens wilt two days after you buy them](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/08/why-do-my-greens-wilt-two-days-after-i.html) covers the moisture side.

## Setting the crisper sliders

The slider is a vent. Samsung's guide uses the high setting for leafy greens and anything that wilts or loses moisture easily, with broccoli, lettuce, cucumbers, peppers, carrots, strawberries, and herbs as its examples. The low setting is for produce that's less sensitive to moisture loss: apples, pears, mangoes, papayas, cantaloupes, and avocados.

So the vegetable drawer goes on high and the fruit drawer goes on low. Brands differ on a few borderline items, so check your manual's chart if it has one.

**No sliders?** UC Davis's method works in any drawer. Keep refrigerated produce in perforated plastic bags. To make your own, poke about 20 pinholes in a medium-size bag. UC Davis also keeps fruit in one drawer and vegetables in the other, sliders or not.

## Potatoes: why the pantry beats the fridge

Iowa State University Extension gives the two limits. Below 40°F, potatoes develop a sugary, sweet taste. Above 50°F, they may sprout within two to three months. A fridge running 38 to 42°F sits right on the low line.

Cold storage matters for more than taste. The FDA says storing potatoes in the refrigerator can increase acrylamide, a chemical that forms during cooking. It recommends keeping potatoes outside the fridge in a dark, cool place such as a closet or pantry.

Three more details from the sources:

- **Dark.** Light turns potatoes green, says Iowa State. UC Davis also tells you to protect them from light.
- **Away from fruit.** Iowa State says not to store potatoes with apples or other fruit, because ethylene from ripening fruit promotes sprouting. The same goes for winter squash.
- **Ventilated.** UC Davis puts potatoes, sweet potatoes, onions, and garlic in a well-ventilated spot in the pantry.

Onions and garlic are a different story. Iowa State's long-term storage temperature for cured garden onions is 32 to 40°F, not a warm range like the one for squash or sweet potatoes. If yours keep sprouting anyway, see [why onions and garlic keep sprouting indoors](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/09/why-onions-and-garlic-keep-sprouting.html).

## Ripening on purpose: a worked example

UC Davis's ripening trick is one ripe apple for every 5 to 7 pieces of fruit, in a bowl or paper bag. The apple's ethylene speeds the others along.

Say you bought 12 hard pears for the weekend:

- 12 pears ÷ 6 pieces per apple (the middle of 5–7) = 2 apples
- At the edges of the range: 12 ÷ 7 ≈ 1.7, or 12 ÷ 5 = 2.4, so 2 apples covers it either way

Two caveats from the same guide. Fuji and Granny Smith apples produce little ethylene, so they won't help. And don't seal the fruit in a plastic bag on the counter. A sealed bag slows ripening, and carbon dioxide builds up inside, which can cause off-odors and decay.

Once the pears ripen, move them to the fruit drawer. UC Davis says ripe Bartlett pears keep 1 to 3 days in the fridge without losing taste. To *slow* an avocado or peach, it's the same move: the fridge, once it's ripe.

## How long refrigerated produce really lasts

UC Davis's advice is to use refrigerated fruits and vegetables within a few days, because longer storage costs freshness and flavor. Counter produce keeps a few days too. Out of direct sun, a vented bowl or perforated bag cuts moisture loss.

If you're regularly losing produce past that window, the fix is usually buying less at once. Pairing storage with a [30-minute Sunday meal plan](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/07/the-30-minute-sunday-meal-plan-that.html) helps match what you buy to what you'll cook that week.

## Washing and cut produce

UC Davis says to wash whole produce under running water just before you prepare it, not before storing it. Soaking increases the chance of cross-contamination. The FDA says bagged greens labeled "ready to eat," "washed," or "triple washed" don't need re-washing at all.

Once produce is cut or peeled, it goes in the fridge promptly. The USDA's rule for perishable food is no more than 2 hours at room temperature, or 1 hour above 90°F. USDA's leftover guidance also assumes a fridge at 40°F or below. Food that has sat out too long isn't safe even when it smells and looks fine, because the bacteria that cause food poisoning generally don't change how food looks or smells.

## FAQ

### Can I put ripe tomatoes in the fridge to buy a few days?

You can, at a cost. Purdue Extension says red tomatoes can be stored at 50°F with some loss of flavor and aroma, and a fridge is colder than that. UC Davis says even red tomatoes lose flavor in the fridge. If you do chill them, use them soon.

### Is it okay to keep bananas with other fruit?

Bananas give off ethylene once fully ripe, says Penn State, so a ripe bunch will speed up other fruit near it. That's useful in a ripening bowl and unhelpful next to fruit you want to hold. They stay out of the fridge, where UC Davis says the skin turns black.

### Why do my carrots taste bitter?

Ethylene exposure is one cause. Penn State lists carrots and parsnips among the vegetables that turn bitter when stored near ethylene-producing fruit. Keep them in the vegetable drawer, away from apples and pears.
