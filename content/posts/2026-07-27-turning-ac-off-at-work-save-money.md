Yes — turning your AC off (or setting it back) while you're at work saves money. The real question is how much, and whether "off" beats a smarter middle option.

The honest answer: raising your thermostat 7-10°F for the 8 hours you're gone typically saves **5-15% off your cooling costs** for the season, according to the general guidance most energy utilities give. Turning it off completely saves a bit more, but the difference is often smaller than people expect — and in humid climates, going fully off can backfire. Here's the full breakdown, checklist-style, so you can figure out which approach fits your house.

## The Core Logic: Why "Off Time" Saves Money At All

**It works because your AC only spends energy fighting the gap between outdoor and indoor temperature.** The bigger that gap, the harder (and longer) your system runs. Let it drift up while you're out, and you shrink the number of hours it's working against a big gap.

- **Your house is a cooler, not a freezer.** It doesn't need to hold 72°F every minute of the day — it just needs to feel right when you're using the rooms. Empty rooms don't care about the temperature.
- **Every degree matters less than the hours matter.** A 2-degree setback for 8 hours saves less than an 8-degree setback for 8 hours, but both save something. Duration and gap size both count.
- **This is the same principle behind the "right settings for every season" idea** covered in general thermostat guidance — you're just applying it specifically to the workday gap instead of overnight or vacation.

## Where "Off" Beats a Setback

- **Hot, dry climates (Southwest, interior California, parts of the Mountain West).** Low humidity means your house won't get swampy or musty while the AC's off, and the temperature swing is manageable. Full shutoff makes sense here.
- **Well-insulated, newer homes.** Good insulation and tight ductwork mean the house heats up slowly during the day and cools back down fast when you get home. You're not fighting a losing battle either direction.
- **Anyone with a programmable or smart thermostat.** You can schedule the exact minute it kicks back on before you walk in, so "off" doesn't mean walking into a sauna. This removes most of the downside.

**Key takeaway: full shutoff saves the most, but only pays off cleanly when humidity and insulation are on your side.**

## Where a Setback (Not Full Off) Is the Smarter Move

- **Humid climates (Southeast, Midwest, Mid-Atlantic, most of Canada in summer).** Turn the AC fully off for 8+ hours and indoor humidity climbs — that's how you get musty smells, sticky floors, and in bad cases, mold starting on walls or in closets. A setback to around 78-80°F keeps the dehumidifying function running at a lower level instead of stopping it.
- **Homes with finished basements or humidity-sensitive rooms.** Instruments, wood furniture, hardwood floors, and anything stored in a basement can warp or mildew faster when humidity swings wildly. A moderate setback avoids the whiplash.
- **Homes with pets home all day.** Dogs and cats handle warm way better than people assume, but extreme heat (85°F+) for 8 hours in a closed house is a real risk, especially for older or short-nosed breeds. A setback to the high 70s keeps things safe without paying for full comfort-cooling.
- **Older AC units or older homes with poor insulation.** If your system has to work overtime to bring the temperature back down from a full shutoff, you can eat into some of the savings with a longer, harder recovery run. A moderate setback is gentler on an aging compressor.

## The Recovery Cost People Forget About

This is the part that trips people up: **bringing a hot house back down to comfortable isn't free**, and in some cases it costs more per hour than the AC would have cost just idling at a normal setpoint.

- **The "recovery run" is longer and harder than normal cycling.** Instead of short bursts to maintain 72°F, your AC runs continuously for sometimes an hour or more to claw back 8-10 degrees, especially on a 95°F day.
- **Oversized swings can strain older systems.** A unit already working near its limit may run flat-out for an extended recovery period, which is exactly the kind of stress that shortens compressor life over years of repeated use.
- **A smart or programmable thermostat erases most of this downside.** Schedule the recovery to start 30-45 minutes before you're due home instead of the second you turn the key — you get a comfortable house without the shock-cooling.

## What Actually Determines Your Savings Number

**There's no single percentage that applies to every house — it depends on a short list of specific factors, and you can estimate your own savings by walking through them:**

1. **Local climate and how hot it gets during your work hours.** A setback in Phoenix during a 105°F afternoon saves more raw dollars than the same setback in Seattle, simply because the AC is working harder to begin with.
2. **How many hours the house is actually empty.** A typical 8-9 hour workday earns more savings than a household where someone's home by early afternoon.
3. **Insulation and window quality.** Homes with older single-pane windows or minimal attic insulation heat up faster while you're gone, which somewhat offsets savings — you're paying more for recovery even though you saved during the day. (This overlaps with the caulking and sealing work covered in general home-sealing guidance — a well-sealed house makes any thermostat strategy work better.)
4. **Your electricity rate structure.** If your utility charges more during peak afternoon hours (common with time-of-use plans), letting the AC sit idle during those exact hours saves more than the percentage alone suggests, because you're avoiding the most expensive kilowatt-hours of the day.
5. **The size and efficiency of your AC system.** A newer, right-sized system recovers faster and cheaper than an old, oversized, or undersized one struggling to keep up.

## A Simple Decision Checklist

Run through this before deciding what to do at 8 a.m. tomorrow:

- [ ] **Do I have a programmable or smart thermostat?** If yes, lean toward full shutoff or an aggressive setback — you control the recovery timing.
- [ ] **Is my climate humid in summer?** If yes, use a setback (78-80°F) instead of full off, to avoid mold and mustiness.
- [ ] **Is anyone or any pet home during the day?** If yes, don't go above roughly 80-82°F — comfort and safety outrank marginal savings.
- [ ] **Is my AC unit old or already struggling to keep up on hot days?** If yes, use a moderate setback rather than full shutoff to avoid a brutal recovery cycle.
- [ ] **Do I have a time-of-use electricity plan?** If yes, prioritize keeping the AC off or high during peak pricing hours specifically, even more than the total number of hours.
- [ ] **Is my home well-sealed and insulated?** If yes, you can push the setback further since the house won't overheat too fast or take forever to recover.

## The Bottom Line Number

For most North American homes, a **7-10°F daytime setback for 8 hours saves somewhere in the ballpark of 5-15% off cooling costs** for the season — real money over a summer, typically landing in the range of a modest but noticeable dent in the monthly bill during peak months. Full shutoff can push that figure a bit higher, but the gap between "off" and "smart setback" is usually smaller than the gap between "doing nothing" and "doing either one."

**The habit matters more than the exact number.** If you're already covering the no-cost basics — closing blinds on the sun-facing side, running ceiling fans, sealing obvious drafts — a daytime setback stacks on top of those savings rather than replacing them.

## FAQ

### Should I turn my AC all the way off or just raise the temperature?
For most humid North American climates, raise it (a setback to 78-80°F) rather than shutting it off completely. You avoid humidity buildup and get an easier recovery. In dry climates with a smart thermostat, full shutoff is fine and saves slightly more.

### How many degrees should I raise the thermostat while I'm at work?
A widely used rule of thumb is 7-10°F above your normal comfort setting for the hours you're away. Going higher saves a little more but increases recovery time and risk of humidity issues in damp climates.

### Will turning the AC off shorten its lifespan?
Not from occasional daily cycling — that's normal. The concern is with very old or undersized units that struggle through long, hard recovery runs; in those cases, a moderate setback is gentler than a full shutoff-and-blast-cool cycle.

### Is it cheaper to leave the AC running all day at a lower level than to cycle it?
No — for most homes, letting the temperature drift up while you're gone and recovering when you're back still beats holding a steady low temperature all day, because you're not paying to cool empty rooms for hours at a time.
