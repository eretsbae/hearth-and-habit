Your toilet runs for a few seconds every couple of minutes because water is slowly leaking out of the tank, and the fill valve keeps topping it back off. It's almost always the flapper.

That short, ghostly refill cycle is different from the classic "running toilet" that hisses nonstop until you jiggle the handle. This one is intermittent — quiet for a while, then a brief gurgle or hiss, then quiet again. It's easy to ignore because it stops on its own. But "stops on its own" doesn't mean "fixed itself." It means the tank is slowly losing water to the bowl (or, less often, losing water somewhere else), and the fill valve is doing exactly what it's designed to do: topping the tank back up to the right level.

**The key takeaway:** an intermittent, self-stopping run is a leak-and-refill cycle, not a stuck valve, and the fix depends on where the leak actually is. Most of the time that's a $6–$15 part and 15 minutes of work. Occasionally it points to something bigger.

## Why This Is Different From a Constantly Running Toilet

If you've already read our guide on fixing a running toilet in 15 minutes, that post covers the toilet that never really stops — water hissing continuously because the fill valve won't shut off, usually thanks to a bad float or fill valve.

This is a related but distinct problem. Here, the fill valve *does* shut off completely. The tank refills, reaches the right level, and stops. Then, minutes later, it dips just low enough to trigger the fill valve again for a few seconds. That's the signature of a slow leak somewhere in the tank, not a valve that's stuck open.

Think of it like a bathtub with the drain cracked open slightly: you can keep the water level steady by adding a trickle now and then, but you're never truly "full and done." Your toilet's fill valve is doing that trickle-topping automatically.

## The Three Places the Leak Is Actually Coming From

Not every intermittent-fill toilet has the same cause. Here's how to think through the options before you start replacing parts.

### 1. The Flapper (most likely, by far)

The flapper is the rubber (or foam-rubber) disc at the bottom of the tank that seals the drain hole between flushes. Over time — typically 3 to 5 years, faster if you use bleach tablets in the tank — it warps, cracks, or grows a thin film of mineral buildup along the seal. Water seeps past it into the bowl, the tank level drops an inch or so, and the fill valve kicks on to compensate.

**How to confirm it:** Take the lid off the tank. Drop a few drops of food coloring (or a dye tablet) into the tank water, then don't flush. Wait 10–15 minutes without touching anything. If color shows up in the bowl, water is leaking past the flapper. This is the single most useful diagnostic step, and it costs nothing.

- **Pros of it being the flapper:** cheap fix, no tools beyond your hands, most home centers carry universal replacements for under $15.
- **Cons:** you need the right shape and size (2-inch vs. 3-inch flappers aren't interchangeable), and cheap universal flappers sometimes warp again within a year or two — buying a slightly better one (with a stainless steel ring instead of all rubber) tends to last longer.

### 2. The Fill Valve or Float Assembly

Less common, but possible: the fill valve itself has an internal leak, or the float is set so the shutoff point is right at the edge of overflowing into the overflow tube. In this case, the tank isn't losing water to the bowl — it's very slowly draining a trickle through the overflow tube, which looks and sounds almost identical to a flapper leak.

**How to confirm it:** Do the dye test above. If the water in the bowl *doesn't* change color, but you can still hear or see water trickling into the overflow tube (the open tube in the center of the tank) after the tank has "finished" filling, the float is set too high or the fill valve has a worn washer.

- **Pros of this being the cause:** float height is a free, 2-minute adjustment (turn the adjustment screw or clip down slightly).
- **Cons:** if the fill valve itself is worn internally, adjustment won't help — you'll need a full fill valve replacement, which is a slightly bigger job (shutting off the supply line, draining the tank, more parts) than swapping a flapper, though still well within most DIYers' range.

### 3. The Supply Line or Tank Bolts (less common, worth ruling out)

If neither the flapper nor the float seems to be the issue, check for dampness around the base of the tank, the bolts connecting tank to bowl, or the supply line fitting. A very slow drip here can cause the fill valve to cycle briefly, especially if the leak is inside the tank bolts (which you won't see from outside).

- **Pros of this diagnosis:** once found, it's usually a simple bolt-tightening or washer replacement.
- **Cons:** it's the hardest of the three to spot, since the moisture can be subtle or hidden, and over-tightening tank bolts can crack the porcelain — snug is the goal, not maximum torque.

## What to Do Once You've Identified the Cause

If it's the flapper, buy a replacement that matches your toilet brand if you can (Kohler, American Standard, and Toto all have slightly different flapper shapes), or a universal adjustable flapper if you're not sure. Turn off the water supply at the shutoff valve behind the toilet, flush to drain most of the tank, unhook the old flapper from its mounting pegs and the chain from the flush lever, and hook up the new one in the same spot. Turn the water back on and test.

If it's the float, most modern fill valves have either a screw you turn (older Fluidmaster-style) or a clip you slide (float-cup style) to lower the shutoff point about half an inch to an inch below the top of the overflow tube. That small drop is often enough to stop the trickle entirely.

If it's the fill valve itself, replacement kits are inexpensive and come with instructions specific to that part — this is a reasonable weekend project even if you've never done it, but budget a bit more time than the flapper swap.

## When the Intermittent Run Isn't Worth Ignoring

A toilet that runs for a few seconds every 5–10 minutes might not seem urgent, but a slow leak like this can waste a meaningful amount of water over a month — often enough to show up as a noticeably higher water bill, even though nothing is obviously wrong. It's also one of the most common "invisible" water-waste issues in a home, right alongside the fully running toilet.

It's worth fixing within a week or two of noticing it, not because it's an emergency, but because the parts are cheap and the problem only gets worse — flappers don't heal themselves, and a slightly worn seal today is a constantly running toilet in a few months.

## When to Call a Plumber Instead

Most of this is DIY territory. Call a plumber if:

- You've replaced the flapper and fill valve and the toilet still runs intermittently.
- You see water pooling around the base of the toilet itself (that's a wax ring or seal issue, a different repair entirely).
- The tank or bowl has a hairline crack — no amount of new hardware fixes that.
- Your shutoff valve behind the toilet is seized or won't turn, which needs to be addressed before any tank work.

## FAQ

### Is a toilet that runs for a few seconds every few minutes wasting a lot of water?

It adds up more than people expect. Even a small, intermittent leak can waste dozens of gallons a day, since it's happening around the clock. It's rarely as dramatic as a fully running toilet, but it's not nothing either — worth fixing within a couple of weeks.

### Can I just ignore it if it's not bothering me?

You can, but the flapper or valve that's causing it will typically get worse, not better, over time. What starts as a 5-second trickle every 10 minutes often turns into a constantly running toilet within a few months.

### Does the dye test work with any food coloring?

Yes — a few drops of standard food coloring or a leak-detection dye tablet (sold at hardware stores) both work fine. The key is waiting at least 10 minutes without flushing before you check the bowl.

### Could hard water be causing this?

Indirectly, yes. Mineral buildup from hard water is one of the more common reasons flappers and fill valve seals wear out faster than they should, since deposits keep the seal from closing fully flush.
