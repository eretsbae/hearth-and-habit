Most likely culprit: a rate increase or seasonal weather swing, not you — but appliance problems can double a bill too, so it pays to check both.

A bill that suddenly doubles feels personal, like the utility company is out to get you. Usually it isn't. It's almost always one of a handful of specific things, and each one leaves a different fingerprint on your bill. This guide walks through the most common situations so you can match your symptoms to the cause instead of guessing.

## First: Pull Up Your Actual Usage, Not Just the Total

Before troubleshooting anything, log into your utility's website or app and look at your kilowatt-hour (kWh) usage — the actual amount of electricity you used — not just the dollar amount. This one step tells you which of two very different problems you have:

- **Usage stayed about the same, but the bill went up.** That's a rate or pricing issue.
- **Usage itself roughly doubled.** That's a consumption issue — something in your home is actually drawing more power.

Most utilities show a 12-month usage graph. Pull it up and see if this month sticks out compared to the same month last year, not just last month.

## If Your Usage (kWh) Barely Changed but the Bill Doubled

This is a pricing problem, and it's more common than people think, especially with recent years' energy price volatility.

**Check for a rate increase.** Utilities periodically raise rates, and many pass through changes in fuel costs without much fanfare — sometimes just a line in a mailed notice you skimmed past. Compare the "price per kWh" line on this bill to an old bill from 6-12 months ago. A jump from, say, 12 cents to 20 cents per kWh will double your bill with zero change in behavior.

**Check if you fell off a promotional or fixed-rate plan.** If you're on a deregulated market (common in states like Texas or parts of the Northeast, and in some Canadian provinces), a 12- or 24-month fixed rate often expires and rolls into a much higher variable "market rate." This is one of the single biggest causes of bill-doubling, and it's easy to miss because nothing about your house changed.

**Check for new fees or a delivery charge increase.** Some bills separate "supply" (the electricity itself) from "delivery" (getting it to your house). Delivery charges can rise even when supply rates don't.

**What to do:** Call your utility or retail electricity provider and ask directly: "Did my rate per kWh change, and why?" If you're on an expired promotional plan, ask about switching to a new fixed-rate plan — don't just let it keep floating at the market rate.

## If Your Usage Genuinely Doubled — Match the Season First

If the kWh number itself jumped, weather is the first thing to rule out, especially with electric heating or cooling.

**If this happened in the dead of summer or the coldest weeks of winter:** this is very often just the AC or electric heat working harder than usual. Air conditioners and electric resistance heaters (baseboard heat, space heaters, electric furnaces) are enormous power draws — often more electricity than everything else in the house combined. A stretch of unusually hot or cold weather compared to last year can genuinely double a bill on its own, with no equipment problem at all. **If it happened in a mild-weather month (spring or fall) when AC and heat should barely be running:** weather is probably not the explanation, and you should look at equipment instead — see the sections below.

## If It's Summer and the Doubling Lines Up With AC Use

Check these before assuming something's broken:

- **Air filter.** A clogged furnace/AC filter makes the system run much longer to move the same amount of air. This is the single most common, most overlooked cause of a summer spike. (See our furnace filter guide for how often to actually change it.)
- **Thermostat habits.** If you started leaving the AC running at the same setting all day instead of letting the house drift a few degrees while empty, that adds up fast — we've written a full breakdown of whether turning the AC off at work actually saves money.
- **Outdoor unit airflow.** Overgrown grass, leaves, or debris packed around the outdoor condenser unit forces it to work harder. A few minutes of clearing space around it can restore normal efficiency.
- **Aging AC unit.** Units over 12-15 years old lose efficiency gradually, then sometimes fall off a cliff in one hot season. If the unit is old and the electric bill jump was dramatic and sudden, a failing capacitor or dirty coil (not just old age) could be forcing it to run constantly — worth a technician's look if it's not cooling as well as before despite running more.

## If It's Winter and You Have Any Electric Heat

Electric heat — baseboards, space heaters, an electric furnace, or a heat pump running its backup "emergency heat" strip — is far more expensive per hour than gas heat.

**The single biggest hidden cause here:** a heat pump stuck in backup/emergency heat mode. Heat pumps are efficient in mild cold, but below roughly 25-35°F (varies by model) many switch to electric resistance backup heat, which is dramatically more expensive to run. If a heat pump has a stuck sensor, a refrigerant issue, or was accidentally switched to "emergency heat" on the thermostat, it can run on expensive backup heat for weeks and quietly double a bill. Check the thermostat display for an "EM HEAT" or similar indicator.

**Also check:** whether anyone in the house started using a space heater regularly. A single 1500-watt space heater running most of the day can add real money to a monthly bill — often more than people expect from "just one little heater."

## If It Happened in a Mild Month With No Obvious Trigger

This is the case worth taking seriously, because it suggests either a meter/billing issue or a genuine appliance problem.

**Rule out an estimated bill correction first.** If a meter reader couldn't access your meter for a month or two, some utilities estimate your usage, then true it up later with an actual reading. If last month's bill looks suspiciously low and this month's is high, they may simply be evening out — check the "estimated" vs. "actual" label on recent bills.

**Then look for one appliance running constantly:**

- **Water heater:** A failing thermostat or heating element can cause an electric water heater to run far more than normal. If your water has also seemed hotter than usual or the tank is old, this is worth checking.
- **Refrigerator:** A fridge with a bad door seal, a coil caked in dust, or a failing compressor can double its own power draw — and a fridge already runs 24/7, so extra inefficiency compounds all day, every day. Torn gaskets and dust-caked coils are common, fixable causes.
- **Well pump, pool pump, or sump pump:** if you have one, a stuck float switch or a pump that's lost its prime can run continuously instead of cycling.
- **Something left on by accident:** a space heater in a rarely-used room, a garage heater, or an EV charger someone set to a less efficient schedule.

**A practical way to isolate this without any tools:** most smart meters let you view hourly usage. Look at 2-3 AM, when nothing should be running except the fridge and any always-on equipment. If overnight usage is unusually high, something is running continuously that shouldn't be — that's your appliance problem, not a weather or rate problem.

**Key takeaway:** match your bill to a category — rate change, seasonal weather, or a specific appliance — using your kWh history and hourly data before assuming anything is broken or before calling the utility to complain.

## FAQ

### Could my electric meter just be wrong?
It's possible but less common than people assume. If you suspect it, note your meter's reading yourself for a few days and compare it to what the utility bills — most utilities will also send someone to test a meter on request, usually for a small fee refunded if the meter turns out faulty.

### Why did my bill double even though I was away for part of the month?
This usually points to something running while you were gone — a water heater, sump pump, or HVAC system left on a normal (not vacation) setting — or it's an estimated-bill correction catching up from a prior month.

### Is it normal for bills to double between winter and summer?
In homes with electric heat or older AC systems, seasonal swings of 1.5x to 2x are common and not a sign of a problem — it reflects how much more electricity heating and cooling use compared to lights and appliances.

### What's the fastest way to tell if it's a rate issue or a usage issue?
Compare the kWh usage number (not the dollar total) to the same month last year. Nearly identical kWh with a higher bill means rates changed; a higher kWh number means something in the house is using more power.
