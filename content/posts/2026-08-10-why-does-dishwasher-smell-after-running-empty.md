Running it empty doesn't clean the machine itself — it just washes air. The smell is almost always trapped food residue, standing water, or mildew hiding in a spot the spray arms never reach.

A dishwasher is really just a small, warm, damp box that food particles pass through several times a day. Left unchecked, that combination turns into the perfect environment for bacteria and mold to settle in. Here's where to check, in the order that tends to solve the problem fastest.

## Start With the Filter (This Fixes Most Smells)

The filter is a removable cylindrical or flat mesh piece at the bottom of the tub, usually near the back, and it's the single most common cause of dishwasher odor. It catches food bits so they don't clog the drain or get re-sprayed onto your dishes — but that means it's holding onto exactly the stuff that smells.

**Key takeaway: if you clean nothing else on this list, clean the filter, and the smell will likely disappear within one cycle.**

- Twist and lift it out (check your manual if it doesn't budge — some lock with a quarter-turn).
- Rinse it under hot water and scrub with an old toothbrush to knock loose the film of grease and food.
- Look underneath it too; debris often collects in the well the filter sits in.

Most manufacturers recommend doing this every month or so, more often if you run the dishwasher daily or skip rinsing dishes before loading.

## Check the Drain and Air Gap

If the filter is clean but the smell lingers, water may not be draining fully after each cycle. Standing water at the bottom of the tub — even a shallow layer — will turn stagnant and sour within a day or two.

- Look for a small cylindrical cap on your countertop near the sink (the air gap); it can clog with gunk and cause slow draining.
- Check that the drain hose isn't kinked, especially if the dishwasher was recently moved or the disposal was serviced.
- If the dishwasher shares a drain line with your garbage disposal, run the disposal and clear any buildup there — a clogged disposal backs smell straight into the dishwasher.

If water is genuinely pooling at the bottom after a full cycle, that's a drainage problem worth addressing before it strains the pump — not just a smell issue.

## Wipe Down the Door Gasket and Edges

The rubber seal around the door traps moisture and food splatter in a spot most people never touch. It's soft, slightly textured, and stays damp between cycles, which makes it a favorite spot for mildew.

- Run a damp cloth or old toothbrush along the entire gasket, including the folds.
- Check the bottom edge of the door and the lip just below it — this is a common blind spot where grime collects and drips go unnoticed.
- Dry it with a towel afterward if you want to slow down regrowth between cleanings.

## Inspect the Spray Arms

The spray arms are the rotating bars with small holes that shoot water onto your dishes. Food particles and mineral deposits can plug those holes, which weakens the wash and lets residue build up elsewhere in the tub over time.

- Spin each arm by hand to make sure it moves freely.
- Use a toothpick or thin wire to clear any visibly clogged holes.
- Rinse the arms under the faucet if they lift out easily on your model.

This one doesn't always cause smell directly, but a weak wash means less residue gets rinsed away each cycle, which compounds the problem everywhere else.

## Run a Real Cleaning Cycle (Not Just an Empty Rinse)

An empty "wash" cycle uses your regular detergent, which is designed to clean dishes, not descale and deodorize the machine itself. That's why it doesn't touch the film of grease and hard-water buildup coating the tub interior.

- Place a cup of plain white vinegar on the top rack and run a hot cycle; this helps cut grease film and mild mineral buildup.
- Follow with a cycle using a sprinkle of baking soda across the bottom of the tub for extra deodorizing.
- Or use a dishwasher-specific cleaning tablet (sold near the detergent) roughly once a month — these are formulated to dissolve grease and limescale that vinegar alone won't fully clear.

Do this monthly as maintenance, not just when you notice a smell. It's the dishwasher equivalent of the seasonal upkeep tasks covered in [The 10 Home Maintenance Tasks Most People Forget](#) — easy to skip, cheap to keep up with.

## Reconsider How You're Loading It

If you've cleaned everything above and the smell keeps coming back within days, loading habits may be feeding it faster than you can clean it out.

- Scrape off large food chunks before loading — you don't need to pre-rinse spotless, but bones, seeds, and big scraps overwhelm the filter fast.
- Don't let dirty dishes sit inside the closed dishwasher for two or three days waiting for a full load; that's a warm, sealed environment doing exactly what it's good at.
- Crack the door open an inch or two after each cycle so the tub can air out and dry instead of staying damp and sealed shut.

If your loading pattern needs a bigger rethink, that's covered in more depth in **Your Dishwasher Is Probably Loaded Wrong** — worth a look if this is a recurring issue and not just a one-time smell.

## Check for Hard Water Buildup

In areas with hard water (water high in minerals like calcium and magnesium), a chalky white film can coat the tub, spray arms, and heating element over time. This buildup traps food residue and grease against it, and it can carry a musty smell even after a normal cleaning.

- Look for white, crusty deposits on the interior walls or around the heating element (usually a coil or flat bar at the bottom).
- If you see heavy buildup, run a citric-acid-based dishwasher cleaner or a few cycles with vinegar until it clears.
- A rinse-aid product, used regularly, helps prevent new mineral film from forming and improves drying too.

This is more common in homes that haven't addressed hard water elsewhere — if you're also noticing spots on glassware or scale on faucets, the dishwasher isn't the only appliance affected.

## When It's Not the Dishwasher at All

Occasionally the smell isn't coming from inside the machine. A sulfur or rotten-egg smell, in particular, can point elsewhere.

- **Garbage disposal**: run it with ice cubes and a little dish soap; smell often travels up through the shared drain.
- **P-trap under the sink**: if the kitchen sink is rarely used, the water in the trap can evaporate, letting sewer gas smell drift up and get blamed on the dishwasher.
- **New plastic or rubber parts**: a brand-new dishwasher can have a mild chemical smell for the first several cycles — this fades on its own and isn't a sign of a problem.

## FAQ

### How often should I actually clean my dishwasher to prevent smell?
A quick filter rinse every 2-4 weeks and a full vinegar-or-cleaner cycle roughly once a month is enough for most households. Homes that run the dishwasher daily or skip scraping food off dishes may need to do it a bit more often.

### Is it safe to use bleach to get rid of dishwasher smell?
Skip it if your dishwasher has a stainless steel interior or a rubber gasket — bleach can damage stainless finishes and degrade rubber seals over time. Vinegar, baking soda, or a citric-acid dishwasher cleaner are safer choices for most machines.

### Why does my dishwasher smell like rotten eggs specifically?
That sulfur smell is usually bacteria buildup in the filter or drain, though it can also be sewer gas from a dry P-trap under the sink. Clean the filter first, and if the smell continues even when the dishwasher isn't running, check the sink drain.

### Can a dishwasher smell mean something is actually broken?
Usually not — smell is almost always a cleaning issue, not a mechanical one. The exceptions are a failing drain pump (water won't drain at all) or a cracked hose leaking near the base, both of which come with visible pooling water, not just odor.
