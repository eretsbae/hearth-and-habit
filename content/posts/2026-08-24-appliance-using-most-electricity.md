In most homes, the biggest electricity users are heating and cooling equipment and, if you have one, an electric water heater — each typically thousands of kWh a year — while a fridge, clothes dryer, or TV each uses somewhere between about 100 and 900 kWh.

That surprises most people, because the appliances that *feel* like energy hogs — a TV left on, a phone charger left plugged in — are usually small potatoes. If you've read about [what phantom power really costs](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/07/phantom-power-how-much-your-idle.html), you know those add up to real but modest money. The heavy hitters are anything that has to fight physics: heating water, heating air, or cooling air. Here's what's typical, and a checklist to find the biggest users in your own home.

## Typical Yearly Electricity Use by Appliance

These are typical ranges, not measurements of your house — actual use depends on climate, household size, and the age of each appliance. Dollar figures use 17¢ per kWh, roughly the U.S. residential average; rates run from about 11¢ to over 40¢ by state, so scale to the rate on your bill.

| Appliance | Typical kWh per year | Typical cost per year at 17¢/kWh |
|---|---|---|
| Electric furnace, baseboards, or heat pump backup strips | Highly variable | Often the largest single cost in cold climates |
| Electric resistance water heater | 3,000–4,500 | $510–$765 |
| Central air conditioning | 1,500–4,000 (climate-dependent) | $255–$680 |
| Clothes dryer (electric) | 500–900 | $85–$155 |
| Refrigerator | 350–700 (older units more) | $60–$120 |
| Electric range and oven | 300–600 | $50–$100 |
| LED lighting, whole house | 150–400 | $25–$70 |
| Dishwasher (including water heating) | 150–300 | $25–$50 |
| TV | 100–200 | $17–$34 |
| Clothes washer (motor only) | 50–150 | $9–$26 |

## The Formula: Watts × Hours

To estimate any single appliance, use **watts × hours used ÷ 1,000 × your price per kWh**. For example, a 1,500-watt space heater running 6 hours a day uses 1,500 × 6 ÷ 1,000 = 9 kWh a day — about $1.53 a day at 17¢, or roughly $46 a month.

One caution: the wattage on the label is the *maximum* draw. Appliances that cycle on and off — fridges, water heaters, AC — use far less than label wattage × 24 hours, which is why a watt meter or your utility's hourly data beats multiplying the nameplate.

## The Usual Suspects: Heating and Cooling Appliances

**Central air conditioning and electric heat.** Heating and cooling the house itself dwarfs every individual appliance in most homes. Central AC typically uses 1,500–4,000 kWh a year depending on climate, and electric resistance heat — an electric furnace, baseboards, or a heat pump leaning on its backup strips — is often the single largest electricity use in cold climates. It isn't an "appliance" in the kitchen sense, but it belongs at the top of any honest checklist.

- *Check it*: The habits side is covered in [thermostat settings by season](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/07/the-right-thermostat-settings-for-every.html). The equipment side matters too: an AC unit older than 12–15 years is noticeably less efficient than a modern one.
- *Fix it*: A dirty filter makes the system work harder for the same result — [changing your furnace filter](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/07/furnace-filter-basics-how-often-to.html) on schedule is one of the cheapest fixes on this list.

**Water heater.** If your water heater is electric (not gas), it's usually the biggest single appliance after heating and cooling — typically 3,000–4,500 kWh a year, often somewhere around 15–20% of the electric bill. It runs multiple times a day, every day, and keeping 40–50 gallons hot around the clock takes real energy, even with good insulation. In mild climates, it can out-use the AC.

- *Check it*: Look at the tank temperature setting. Many are set to 140°F (60°C) out of the box; 120°F (49°C) is plenty hot for washing and bathing and cuts standby heat loss noticeably.
- *Fix it*: An insulating water heater blanket (roughly $20–40) helps if the tank feels warm to the touch on the outside. If you're due for a replacement, a heat pump water heater uses a fraction of the electricity of a standard electric resistance tank, though it costs more upfront.

**Clothes dryer.** An electric dryer draws several thousand watts while running — in the same league as a central AC compressor — because it's heating air. A few loads a week adds up to roughly 500–900 kWh a year.

- *Check it*: If your dryer runs long cycles regularly, check the lint trap and the exhaust vent — a clogged one can double drying time and electricity use for the same load of laundry.
- *Fix it*: Clean the lint trap every load, not every few loads. Dry consecutive loads back-to-back so you're not reheating a cold dryer each time.

## The Quiet Ones People Overlook

**Refrigerator.** Unlike a dryer, a fridge doesn't feel like it's working hard, but it runs 24/7, 365 days a year. A second fridge in the garage or basement, especially an older one, can quietly cost more per year than most people would guess.

- *Check it*: Feel the door seals for firm suction. Vacuum the coils on the back or bottom twice a year — dust-caked coils force the compressor to run longer to hit the same temperature.
- *Fix it*: If you're keeping an old second fridge running just for a few drinks and condiments, do the math on whether it's worth it. Units from the 1990s or earlier can use roughly twice the electricity of a new efficient model.

**Electric oven and stovetop.** Ovens draw a lot of power in short bursts, which makes them easy to underestimate — you don't notice an hour of oven use the way you'd notice an AC running all afternoon.

- *Check it*: If you bake or roast several times a week, that's a habitual cost, not a one-off.
- *Fix it*: A toaster oven or air fryer uses meaningfully less electricity than heating a full-size oven for small meals — worth it for reheating or cooking for one or two people.

**Well pump (if you have one).** Often forgotten because it's out of sight, but a well pump working overtime — because of a leak, a failing pressure tank, or a running toilet somewhere in the house — can push electric use up without any obvious sign indoors.

- *Check it*: If your home is on well water, notice if the pump cycles on and off even when no one's using water. That's a sign of a leak worth chasing down, and a running toilet is a common cause.

## Appliances That Get Blamed but Usually Aren't the Problem

**Dishwasher.** People often suspect the dishwasher because it makes noise and uses hot water, but modern dishwashers typically use less hot water and energy than washing the same load by hand under a running tap. At 150–300 kWh a year, it's rarely the source of a bill spike.

**Microwave.** High wattage on the label (often 1,000–1,500 watts) makes it look scary, but it only runs for minutes at a time. Total energy used per week is small compared to anything that runs for hours.

**Chargers and small electronics.** These matter in aggregate, but a single charger left plugged in draws well under a watt — it's not competing with a water heater or dryer. Don't chase these first.

## How to Actually Confirm the Culprit in Your Home

A general table only gets you so far, because every household's mix is different — a family running a well pump and an old chest freezer looks nothing like an apartment with electric baseboard heat. Two low-cost ways to get real answers:

1. **A plug-in watt meter** (roughly $15–30) lets you test individual appliances — plug it between the outlet and the appliance, and it'll show actual power draw and total kWh over time. Great for fridges, freezers, space heaters, and window AC units.
2. **Your utility's usage data.** Many providers now show hour-by-hour or day-by-day electric use online or in an app. Look for spikes that line up with laundry day, oven use, or hot weather — that pattern tells you a lot without buying anything. If your [bill doubled with no obvious change in habits](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/08/why-did-my-electric-bill-double-with-no.html), this is the first place to look before assuming a rate hike.

**Key takeaway: heating, cooling, and an electric water heater dwarf everything else, so start there — use thermostat setbacks, keep the filter clean, and set the water heater to 120°F. All three cost little or nothing.**

## FAQ

### Is my air conditioner or my water heater the bigger cost?
It depends on your climate. An electric resistance water heater typically uses 3,000–4,500 kWh a year, while central AC ranges from about 1,500 kWh in mild climates to 4,000 or more in hot ones — so in the South the AC often wins, and in cooler regions an electric water heater often does. If you heat your home with electricity, that usually tops both. With a gas water heater, the AC is almost always the bigger electric cost.

### Does an old freezer in the garage really cost that much?
It can. An old freezer or fridge can use roughly twice the electricity of a new one, and running it in an unconditioned garage (where it works harder in summer heat) makes it worse. Worth testing with a watt meter if you're unsure it's earning its keep.

### Will unplugging appliances when not in use make a big difference?
It helps more for electronics with standby power (TVs, game consoles, AV gear) than for major appliances. You wouldn't unplug a fridge or water heater — those need to run continuously by design.

### How can I tell if a specific appliance caused a bill spike?
Compare the timing: if your bill jumped in summer, suspect AC; in winter, suspect heating or an electric water heater working harder with colder incoming water. A plug-in watt meter or your utility's hourly usage app can confirm which appliance is actually running longer or drawing more than usual.
