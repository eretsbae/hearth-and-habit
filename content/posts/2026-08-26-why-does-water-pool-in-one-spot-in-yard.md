Water pools in the same spot because that spot is lower than the ground around it, the soil under it doesn't absorb water, or runoff from a downspout or higher ground is being delivered to it. Where the puddle sits and how long it lasts tell you which.

Two numbers anchor the whole diagnosis. Near the house, downspouts should end at least 5 feet out, and the ground should drop about 6 inches over the first 10 feet. Anywhere in the yard, rain should soak in within 24 hours. University of Connecticut rain-garden researchers use that 24-hour line to judge whether soil drains well enough.

Below: a quick way to match your puddle to its cause, three cheap tests, and the fixes, including the topsoil mistake that turns a damp spot into a permanently wet one.

## Match the puddle to the cause

| What you see | Most likely cause | First test | Fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| Puddle within about 10 feet of the house | Downspout dumping close by, or ground sloping toward the house | Measure where the downspout ends; check the slope | Extend downspouts 5+ feet; regrade to about 6 inches of drop over 10 feet |
| Puddle in the open lawn, gone within a few hours | Settled low spot | None needed | Topdress thinly, repeated over months |
| Water still standing after 24 hours | Compacted or slow-draining soil | Pen test, then the 6-hour hole test | Core aeration; don't bury it under mismatched soil |
| You can see water flowing in from higher ground | The yard funnels runoff there | Watch it during a rain | Redirect the flow with a swale, or capture it upslope with a rain garden |
| Puddle appeared suddenly after years of none | Something changed: new grading, heavy equipment, a failed buried line | Look for recent work nearby; check for water when it hasn't rained | Fix the trigger; call a plumber if water shows up in dry weather |

## If the puddle is near the house

Start here, because this is the one that can become a wet basement.

**Downspouts first.** The Department of Energy's building-science guidance says downspouts should end at least 5 feet from the foundation. If you pipe water to an underground catchment system, that should be at least 10 feet away. A downspout that empties at the base of the wall will puddle there every storm, no matter how good the grading is. Check the gutters above too; if they overflow in heavy rain, [gutter guards don't take care of that on their own](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/08/do-you-still-need-to-clean-gutters-if.html).

Where the water goes next matters. The EPA's advice on redirecting downspouts is to send water to a lawn or garden where it can soak in. It also cautions you to check local rules and avoid sending it somewhere that causes damage or other problems, like a neighbor's yard, a walkway that ices over, or a septic field.

**Then the grade.** The usual target is about a 6-inch drop over the first 10 feet out from the foundation. To check it, run a string from a stake at the wall to a stake 10 feet out, level it with a line level, and measure from the string down to the ground at the far stake. Six inches or more means you're fine. Zero, or ground that's higher out there than at the wall, means water is being steered at your house.

Fill near the foundation should slope water away, and it should be firm soil that holds its shape. A thick layer of loose, organic "black dirt" is the wrong choice; see the topsoil warning below.

## If water stands for more than a day

That points to the soil, not the shape of the ground. Two tests sort it out.

**The pen test.** University of Wisconsin–Extension turf specialists offer a simple check: if you can't push a pen into *dry* soil, it's compacted. Try it in the problem spot and in a healthy part of the lawn. Compacted soil limits how much water soaks in and how much oxygen reaches roots.

**The 6-hour hole test.** UConn's homeowner percolation test is the one to use. Call 811 a few business days before you dig, even for a small hole. Then:

1. Dig a hole 6 to 8 inches deep in the spot.
2. Fill it with water.
3. Check after 6 hours, and again at 24 hours.

| Water level at the check | What UConn says it means |
|---|---|
| Gone at 6 hours | Soil drains well |
| Gone between 6 and 24 hours | Probably acceptable, but the soil may need improvement |
| Still there at 24 hours | Soil isn't absorbing water; this is not a spot to send more water |

UConn also notes that new construction often leaves only 2 to 3 inches of topsoil over compacted subsoil. If your house is newer and the whole yard is slow, that's the likely reason.

**The fix for compaction: core aeration.** Wisconsin's guidance is specific about method:

- **Use a hollow-tine (core) aerator** that pulls plugs out, about 3 inches deep where possible.
- **Skip solid tines and spikes.** Solid tines push soil down and may *increase* compaction. Spiked lawn shoes are "totally ineffective."
- **Aerate when the soil is slightly moist.** If it's dry, water thoroughly 1 to 2 days before so the tines can penetrate.
- **Time it to active growth.** Wisconsin's best window is early autumn, with mid-to-late spring as the second choice. In warmer regions, follow your local extension's timing for your grass type.
- **Repeat if needed.** Compacted sites may need aeration up to twice a year for a year or more.

Sandy soils rarely compact, so if yours is sandy and still puddles, look harder at grade and runoff.

## If it's a low spot in the open lawn

A puddle out in the lawn that's gone within hours is a dip, not a drainage failure. It's mostly a cosmetic problem, though grass in a spot that stays saturated can thin out. The fix is to raise the spot gradually.

**Topdress thin layers, not a pile.** University of Wisconsin–Extension defines topdressing as a layer usually no more than ¼ to ½ inch. Keep at least half the height of the grass blades visible, or the grass underneath can die from lack of light. Let the grass grow through, then add the next layer over the following months.

**Worked example: a 2-inch dip, about 200 square feet.**

- Layers needed: 2 inches ÷ ½ inch per layer = **4 applications**, spread over several months of growth.
- Soil per application: 200 sq ft × 0.04 ft (½ inch) = **8 cubic feet**.
- Total: 8 × 4 = 32 cubic feet, or 32 ÷ 27 = about **1.2 cubic yards**.

Large dips can be filled with a shovel and reseeded instead. For gradual smoothing, Wisconsin notes that core aerating once or twice a year helps too, because soil from the cores washes into low spots over several years.

**The topsoil mistake that makes it worse.** Use soil that matches what's already there. If your yard is a silt loam, topdress with silt loam, because mismatched soil creates layers that water won't move through easily. Wisconsin is blunt about the worst choice: an unmixed layer of "black dirt" or muck soil "will prevent water from draining deeper into the soil, resulting in a wet area and often killing the turf." It also advises using compost cautiously, if at all, since some composts seal the soil surface. So the bag of rich, dark fill that looks right can be the thing that turns a damp spot into a permanently wet one.

Seeding into standing water won't work, so fix the low spot first and reseed after. For the lighter-touch maintenance that follows, see our [bare-minimum lawn schedule](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/07/lawn-care-for-people-who-dont-care.html).

## If water runs in from higher ground

When you can watch water travel across the lawn to the puddle, the spot isn't the problem; it's the destination. You have two options: steer the water around the spot with a shallow, grass-lined channel called a swale, or catch it before it gets there.

A **rain garden** catches it: a shallow planted basin that holds runoff from a roof or driveway and lets it soak in. UConn's siting rules keep people out of trouble:

- **Not in the spot that's always wet.** A rain garden needs soil that drains (the 6-hour test above). The chronic puddle usually fails that test, so put the garden upslope where water arrives, not where it collects.
- **At least 10 feet from a house with a basement.**
- **Not over a septic system, near a drinking-water well, or where bedrock is within 2 feet of the surface.**
- **Plan an overflow route** that doesn't lead back toward the foundation.

**Sizing example:** UConn sizes a rain garden to hold the first inch of runoff, using drainage area × 1.3 ÷ 6 for a garden 6 inches deep. If one downspout drains half of a 40 × 30-foot roof, that's 600 square feet. 600 × 1.3 ÷ 6 = **130 square feet** of garden. A properly built one holds water only about 6 hours after a storm, and UConn puts plant costs at around $1 to $2 per square foot.

For water that has to move a long way or go underground, a buried perforated-pipe trench (a French drain) is the heavier option. That's a job where getting the outlet and slope right matters more than the materials, and many homeowners hire it out. Either way, call 811 before any trench.

## Why "a few days" of standing water matters

Beyond dead grass, there's the mosquito clock. The CDC says an *Aedes* mosquito egg takes 7 to 10 days to become an adult. A puddle that's gone by morning gives them no time. One that lingers for days after every storm, especially in a wet stretch, gives them a nursery. That's one more reason the 24-hour line matters; our [mosquito control guide](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/07/mosquito-control-that-actually-works.html) covers the rest. If [mushrooms pop up](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/08/why-are-mushrooms-suddenly-growing-all.html) in the damp spot afterward, that guide explains what they mean and why they're rarely an emergency.

## FAQ

### Does a puddle that only appears in downpours need fixing?

Not necessarily. If it's gone within a few hours and the spot passes the 6-hour hole test, the soil is simply absorbing water slower than a heavy storm delivers it. Watch for the 24-hour line instead: water still there a day later is the sign of a real problem.

### Can I just fill the low spot with a few bags of topsoil?

Only in thin layers, ¼ to ½ inch at a time, with soil that matches your existing soil. One thick layer smothers the grass, and dark organic "black dirt" can hold water and make the spot wetter.

### When should I call a professional?

Call one when water pools against the foundation and fixing the downspouts and grade hasn't solved it. Also call if the fix needs a French drain or major regrading, or if a wet spot shows up when it hasn't rained, which suggests a leaking irrigation or water line.
