Skipping heated dry typically saves only 5 to 20 cents per load — real money over a year, but not a dramatic fix on its own.

That's a small number, and it might feel disappointing if you were hoping for a bigger win. But small numbers add up when they're free, and this is one of the easiest zero-cost habit changes in the whole kitchen. Here's how to figure out what it's actually worth in your house, and how to make the switch without ending up with wet, spotty dishes.

## Step 1: Understand what heated dry actually does

Heated dry uses a heating element (the same one that may help sanitize during the wash cycle) to warm the air inside the tub after the final rinse, speeding up evaporation. Without it, the dishwasher relies on residual heat from hot rinse water plus ambient air, which takes longer but uses no extra electricity to do it.

Some newer, high-efficiency dishwashers don't use a traditional heating element for drying at all. They use a method sometimes called condensation drying, where a strip or panel (often stainless steel) inside the tub cools faster than the dishes, so moisture condenses onto it instead of your plates. If your dishwasher already works this way, the "heated dry" setting you're toggling might use very little extra energy in the first place — which is worth knowing before you assume you'll see a big change.

## Step 2: Find your dishwasher's actual energy use

You don't need to guess. Most dishwashers have an energy label (often a yellow EnergyGuide sticker in the US, or on the manufacturer's spec sheet) that lists estimated annual electricity use in kilowatt-hours. That figure covers water heating, the motor, and drying combined, so it won't isolate the dry cycle specifically — but it gives you a ceiling to work from.

For a more direct answer, check your owner's manual or search the model number online for "energy consumption" or "cycle wattage." Some manuals break out drying as a separate line item, typically somewhere in the range of 0.1 to 0.5 kWh per cycle when heated dry is used, compared to close to zero for air dry.

**The honest math:** at a typical US electricity rate of roughly 12 to 18 cents per kWh, that's about 1 to 9 cents saved per load just from skipping the heating element — with better insulated, newer machines usually landing on the lower end, and older or larger units on the higher end.

## Step 3: Multiply it out before you decide it's not worth it

One load doesn't feel like much. A household that runs the dishwasher once a day, though, is looking at roughly 365 loads a year. At even a modest 5 cents saved per load, that's about $18 a year for changing one setting and touching nothing else. At 10 cents a load, it's closer to $36.

That's not going to replace a bigger project like adjusting your thermostat settings or fixing phantom power draw from idle electronics, but it costs nothing to implement and never expires. It's the kind of habit that's worth stacking with other no-cost changes rather than expecting it to carry your whole electric bill on its own.

## Step 4: Turn off heated dry the right way

Most dishwashers let you disable heated dry either as its own button or as part of choosing an "air dry" or "energy saver" cycle. A few things to check:

- **Look for a dedicated "heated dry" or "heat dry" toggle** on the control panel — it's often a small button you press once, separate from the main cycle selector.
- **If there's no separate button, check the cycle names.** "Normal" or "Auto" often includes heated dry by default, while "Eco" or "Energy Saver" cycles frequently skip it as part of the whole cycle being lower-energy.
- **Check your manual for a "delay dry" or "no heat" option** if your model doesn't label it obviously — some older machines bury the setting in a combination of buttons held together.

## Step 5: Manage the downside — condensation and slow drying

The real cost of skipping heated dry isn't money, it's time and a little bit of dampness. Without added heat, plastic items in particular can stay wet or spotty, since they cool faster than ceramic or metal and hold condensation longer.

A few adjustments make air-drying work better:

1. **Crack the door open a few inches once the cycle ends.** This is the single biggest fix — it lets humid air escape instead of sitting in a sealed, cooling box.
2. **Run the dishwasher when you'll be around to open it**, rather than a cycle that finishes overnight and sits closed for eight hours.
3. **Load plastics on the top rack** away from direct water pooling, and angle bowls and cups so water drains rather than collects.
4. **Use a rinse aid.** This is the one product upgrade that actually matters here — rinse aid changes how water beads on dishes, helping it sheet off instead of sitting in droplets, which noticeably reduces spotting even without heat.

If you already load your dishwasher with an eye for airflow and spacing — the same principles that get dishes cleaner in the wash cycle also help them dry faster afterward — you'll notice less of a difference switching to air dry than someone whose racks are packed tight.

## Step 6: Decide if it's worth it for your household

**Choose air dry if:**
- You're usually home when the cycle finishes and can crack the door.
- Your loads are mostly plates, glasses, and metal utensils rather than lots of plastic.
- You already use rinse aid or don't mind starting to.
- You like stacking small, free savings and don't need this one to be dramatic.

**Stick with heated dry if:**
- You run the dishwasher overnight and want dry dishes waiting in a closed unit by morning.
- Your loads are plastic-heavy (kids' cups, food storage containers) and you've been annoyed by residual moisture before.
- You live somewhere with high humidity, where air-drying inside a closed dishwasher works especially poorly.

There's also a middle option worth knowing about: many dishwashers have a "heated dry" setting that uses a much lower, shorter burst of heat than older sanitizing-style cycles. If yours does, the savings from turning it off will be smaller than the ranges above — closer to a couple of cents a load. Checking your specific model's numbers, rather than assuming, is what turns this from a guess into a real decision.

## The takeaway

**Air-drying is almost always a little cheaper, but rarely dramatically so — usually a matter of pennies per load that adds up to somewhere between $15 and $40 a year for a typical household.** It costs nothing to try, and if damp plastics or slow drying bother you, cracking the dishwasher door and using rinse aid solves most of the downside. Treat it as one small piece of a broader energy-saving routine rather than a fix on its own.

## FAQ

### Does heated dry actually clean dishes better, or just dry them?
It only dries them faster; it isn't part of the cleaning chemistry. Cleaning happens during the wash and rinse cycles with hot water and detergent. Some dishwashers do have a separate "sanitize" cycle that uses higher water temperatures to kill more bacteria, but that's a different setting from heated dry and affects the wash, not just the drying step.

### Will skipping heated dry damage my dishwasher over time?
No. Air dry is a standard, manufacturer-supported setting, not a workaround. There's no wear-and-tear benefit either way — the heating element isn't damaged by being used, and skipping it doesn't extend the dishwasher's life in any meaningful way.

### Is it cheaper to just let dishes air-dry on a rack instead of using the dishwasher at all?
Not necessarily. Hand-washing typically uses more hot water per dish than a modern dishwasher, which is engineered to clean a full load with a fairly small, precise amount of water. If you're already running full loads in the dishwasher, letting it air-dry is usually cheaper and more water-efficient than washing the same dishes by hand.

### Does the "energy saver" cycle just mean no heated dry, or does it change other things too?
It usually changes more than drying. Energy-saver or "Eco" cycles often use lower wash water temperatures and longer cycle times, letting the dishwasher clean effectively while using less electricity to heat water — heated dry being turned off is typically just one part of that bundle, not the whole story.
