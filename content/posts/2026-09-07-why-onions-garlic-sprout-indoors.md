Onions and garlic sprout indoors because they're kept too warm, too humid, or sealed away from air — sprouting means they think spring has arrived. Fix the storage conditions and it mostly stops.

A sprouting onion or garlic bulb isn't spoiled — it's just responding to conditions that mimic the arrival of growing season. Bulbs are built to sense warmth, moisture, and light cues that tell them to wake up. Most kitchens accidentally provide all three. Here's the checklist to walk through, starting with the biggest offenders.

## The Big Three Causes (Check These First)

**Your kitchen is too warm.** Onions and garlic keep best around 60°F (15°C) or cooler, ideally somewhere between 45–60°F. Most kitchens run 68–72°F, especially near the stove, oven, or a sunny window. That's warm enough to signal "time to grow" to a bulb that's biologically ready to sprout the moment temperatures rise.

**There's too much humidity or moisture nearby.** Sprouting is triggered partly by moisture. A bulb stored near a sink, dish rack, damp countertop, or inside a plastic bag that traps condensation gets exactly the cue it's waiting for. Garlic is especially sensitive — it sprouts faster than onions in most kitchens because the cloves are smaller and react quicker to humidity.

**They're getting light exposure.** A little ambient light won't ruin anything, but bulbs stored in a clear bowl on a sunlit counter get a steady light signal that, combined with warmth, speeds up sprouting considerably. Darkness alone won't save a bulb that's too warm, but it removes one more trigger.

**Key takeaway: sprouting is almost never about the onion or garlic being "bad" — it's a storage temperature and airflow problem.**

## Storage Setup Checklist

- **Use a well-ventilated container, not a sealed bag.** Mesh bags, wire baskets, paper bags with a few holes, or a wooden bowl all let air circulate. Plastic bags and airtight containers trap the humidity bulbs release naturally as they respire (yes, they're still slowly "breathing" even sitting on your counter).
- **Pick the coolest, darkest spot that isn't the fridge.** A pantry, a cabinet away from the stove, a basement shelf, or a cool closet all work better than an open countertop. The goal is consistently cool and dry, not cold and damp.
- **Keep onions and potatoes apart.** This one surprises people: potatoes release moisture and a gas that speeds up sprouting in nearby onions. Storing them in the same bin or drawer accelerates the exact problem you're trying to avoid.
- **Don't store garlic and onions pressed tightly together in bulk.** Crowding restricts airflow between bulbs, trapping humidity right where it does the most damage. Give them room to breathe, even if that means a shallower basket instead of a deep bin.
- **Check for one bad bulb hiding among good ones.** A soft or sprouting bulb releases more moisture and ethylene gas, nudging its neighbors toward sprouting too. Sorting through the basket every week or two catches this before it spreads.

## What to Do With Bulbs That Have Already Sprouted

**A few green shoots don't mean the onion or garlic is unsafe to eat.** The bulb is edible as long as it's still firm, without mold, soft spots, or a sour smell. The sprout itself pulls sugars and moisture from the bulb, so flavor and texture do decline the longer it sits, but it isn't a food-safety issue.

- **Use sprouted garlic in cooked dishes first.** The green shoot inside can taste slightly bitter or sharper raw; cooking mellows it out. If the bitterness bothers you, just slice the clove in half and pull the green sprout out before mincing.
- **Sprouted onions are best used soon, not saved for later.** Once the green shoot pushes through the top, the onion is actively drawing energy from its own layers. It'll go soft and papery faster from that point on, so bump it up in your meal plan rather than pushing it to the back of the pantry.
- **Save the green shoots themselves.** Both onion and garlic sprouts are edible and taste similar to scallions or chives — chop them into eggs, soups, or a stir-fry instead of composting them.
- **Don't try to "revive" a bulb by planting it indoors** unless you actually want to grow green onion tops in a windowsill jar. It won't reverse the process for cooking purposes; the bulb itself keeps softening while the green top grows.

## Buying and Rotation Habits That Prevent the Problem Upstream

**Buy loose bulbs instead of pre-bagged mesh sacks when you can choose.** Bagged sets from the grocery store often include one or two bulbs that are already further along in sprouting, and as noted above, one bulb can speed up the rest. Loose bins let you pick firm, dry, tight-skinned bulbs individually.

**Buy in quantities that match how fast you actually cook.** This connects to a broader meal-planning habit worth building: if a weekly plan only calls for half an onion here and there, a 3-pound bag bought "because it was cheaper" is going to sit around long enough to sprout regardless of storage conditions. Smaller, more frequent purchases often beat bulk buying for anything with a shelf life measured in weeks rather than months.

**Rotate stock front-to-back.** Push older bulbs to the front of the basket when you bring new ones home. It sounds obvious, but most sprouting happens to the bulb that got pushed to the back and forgotten for six weeks, not the one bought yesterday.

**Know the realistic shelf life.** Whole, unpeeled onions typically last 1–2 months in good cool, dry storage. Garlic bulbs typically last 3–6 months under the same conditions. Once either is cut, peeled, or sprouting heavily, that timeline shortens to days in the fridge, not weeks on the counter.

## FAQ

### Should I refrigerate onions and garlic to stop sprouting?

Whole, unpeeled bulbs generally do better outside the fridge in a cool, dry, ventilated spot — refrigerator humidity can actually soften them and encourage mold rather than prevent sprouting. The exception is once they're cut, peeled, or already sprouting fast; at that point, refrigerating in a sealed container does slow things down for the short term.

### Why does garlic seem to sprout faster than onions?

Garlic cloves are smaller and have less protective outer layer per clove, so they respond to warmth and humidity more quickly than a larger onion bulb. Bulk garlic (like the pre-peeled jars or nets) also tends to have more surface exposure, which speeds it up further.

### Does it matter what container I use — mesh bag vs. basket vs. bowl?

Mostly what matters is airflow and darkness, not the specific material. A wire basket, a paper bag with holes poked in it, or a wooden bowl in a cabinet all work similarly well. What to avoid is anything sealed (plastic bags, airtight containers) or anything in direct light on a warm counter.

### Can I stop sprouting by storing onions and garlic in the freezer?

Freezing does stop sprouting, but it changes the texture significantly — frozen and thawed onions turn soft and watery, fine for cooked dishes like soups or stews but unusable for anything that needs a crisp bite, like a salad or a sandwich. It's a reasonable move only if you're planning to use them exclusively in cooked meals.
