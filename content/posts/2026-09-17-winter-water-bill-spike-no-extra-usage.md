A winter water bill usually spikes for one of two reasons: water is leaving through a leak you can't see, or the bill itself changed. That can be an estimated reading catching up, a January rate increase, or a sewer charge set from your winter use.

Winter is the season the EPA points to for checking your usage. With little or no outdoor watering, what's left on the meter is mostly indoor use. Below are the EPA's benchmarks for what a household should use in January, a two-hour meter test, and a sewer-billing rule some utilities use that can turn a small winter leak into a year of higher bills.

## Step 1: Compare your gallons with the EPA's winter benchmark

The EPA's leak guidance says to look at your usage in a cold month like January or February. If a family of four uses more than 12,000 gallons in a month, there could be a serious leak.

Your bill may not be in gallons. The EPA lists CCF (hundred cubic feet) as one of the most common billing units, and one CCF is 748 gallons. The EPA's average is 82 gallons per person per day at home. Over 30 days:

| People in the home | 82 gal × people × 30 days | In CCF (÷ 748) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2,460 gallons | about 3.3 |
| 2 | 4,920 gallons | about 6.6 |
| 3 | 7,380 gallons | about 9.9 |
| 4 | 9,840 gallons | about 13.2 |
| Family of four, EPA's leak red flag | over 12,000 gallons | over 16 |

These are national averages. Your own history is the better yardstick, so compare against past bills if your bill or online account shows them. The EPA's bill guide says that if your use rises when you haven't been watering outdoors, it could be a sign of a leak.

## Step 2: Run the two-hour meter test

The EPA's test takes two hours and no tools:

1. Make sure nobody uses water: no flushing, no faucets, no dishwasher or washer, and no ice maker filling if you can pause it.
2. Read the meter and write down every digit.
3. Read it again two hours later. The EPA says that if the meter changed at all, you probably have a leak.

If it moved, start with the toilets. The EPA's dye test is a few drops of food coloring in the tank. If color shows up in the bowl after 10 minutes without flushing, there's a leak, usually the flapper. The EPA says flappers wear out and should be replaced at least every five years. A worn one can silently leak thousands of gallons a year.

Our guide to [fixing a running toilet](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/07/how-to-fix-running-toilet-in-15-minutes.html) covers the swap. A toilet that refills for a few seconds on its own can point to a similar leak; see [why a toilet runs for a few seconds, then stops](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/07/why-does-my-toilet-run-for-few-seconds.html).

Then check faucets, the water heater, and any pipe in an unheated space. Utah State University Extension explains why winter matters here. Water expands as it freezes and can crack or split a pipe. When that line thaws under pressure, it leaks, and a leak inside a wall, under a floor or above a ceiling can be hard to reach and expensive to repair.

## What a leak costs in gallons

The EPA's numbers show leaks are common, not exotic:

- The average household's leaks waste more than 9,300 gallons a year.
- Nine percent of homes have leaks wasting 50 gallons or more a day.
- A faucet dripping once a second wastes more than 3,000 gallons a year.
- Fixing the easy leaks saves about 10 percent on a water bill.

Take a leak at the EPA's 50-gallons-a-day mark:

- 50 gallons × 30 days = 1,500 gallons a month
- 1,500 ÷ 748 = about 2 extra CCF on every bill

For a family of four near the 9,840-gallon average, that alone pushes the month to about 11,340 gallons. Add normal variation and you're close to the EPA's 12,000-gallon red flag.

## Step 3: Read the bill, not just the total

If the meter test is clean, the change is probably on the bill itself. Check three things.

### An estimated reading catching up

Look for "estimated" or "E" next to the reading. State utility rules allow for this in winter. Pennsylvania's rules, which cover water utilities, let a utility estimate a bill when extreme weather prevents a meter reading. Monthly-billed utilities may estimate every other month, but they must let you read the meter yourself and report it instead. If the utility can't get access to the meter, it still needs an actual reading at least once every 12 months.

When the real reading arrives, the gap between the estimates and actual use lands on one bill. If your last few bills were estimated low, that "spike" may be months of water you already used. Read your own meter and call the utility to confirm. The same pattern shows up on power bills, covered in [why an electric bill doubles with no change in usage](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/08/why-did-my-electric-bill-double-with-no.html).

### A January rate change

Some utilities reset rates at the start of the year. Denver Water's 2026 residential rate changes, for example, took effect January 1. Compare the price per unit on this bill with last year's, not just the total.

Also check whether your utility uses tiers. Denver charges residential customers on a tiered structure, where the more you use, the more you pay per unit. The EPA's bill guide calls this an increasing block rate. On a tiered rate, a leak's extra gallons get billed at the highest tier your use reaches.

### A sewer charge set by your winter use

This one is easy to miss. Some utilities set your sewer (wastewater) charge from your winter water use. Austin Water's program shows how it works:

- It averages your water use over a three-month late-fall and winter window.
- That average sets your wastewater volume for the next 12 months. It won't go up no matter how much water you use the rest of the year.
- Austin says conserving during the window lowers the wastewater volume you're billed each month for the next year.
- It also notes that wastewater costs more to treat than water.

Now put the 50-gallon-a-day leak inside that window. Your averaged wastewater volume rises by about 1,500 gallons a month. That gets billed every month for the next year: 1,500 × 12 = 18,000 extra gallons of billed wastewater, even after the leak is fixed.

Check your utility's website or bill to see whether it uses winter averaging. If it does, find out your averaging dates and run the meter test before they start.

## The dripping-faucet trade-off

The Red Cross says to let cold water drip from a faucet served by exposed pipes when it's very cold. A trickle keeps water moving and helps prevent freezing. Utah State University Extension adds the other side: running water works, but it wastes water. It should be the fallback when more permanent fixes like pipe insulation aren't possible. USU also notes that hot water lines freeze too.

For scale, the EPA says a leak of one drip per second wastes more than 3,000 gallons a year, or about 250 gallons a month. A deliberate trickle can run faster than that. Drip only during hard freezes, only at faucets on exposed or outside-wall pipes, and stop once the cold breaks. USU points to more permanent methods such as insulation, though it notes insulation alone won't protect pipes from prolonged freezing without some heat or periodic water flow. Our guide to [the temperature when pipes actually freeze](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/10/at-what-temperature-do-pipes-freeze.html) helps you decide which nights need it.

## FAQ

### Can cold weather make my water meter read high?

The meter test answers that for your home. If the meter doesn't move during two hours of no use, it isn't recording water while none is being used. Then look at the bill for an estimated read or a rate change. Pennsylvania's rules, for example, require utilities with remote-read meters to verify them with an actual reading at least once every five years.

### My bill went up while we were away for the holidays. Why?

Your use should have dropped, so a higher bill points to water running while nobody was there. That might be a toilet flapper, or a pipe that froze and cracked and then leaked after it thawed, which USU warns can happen inside walls. Run the meter test when you're back. Make sure everyone in the house knows where the main shutoff is, as USU advises.

### Is a slightly higher winter bill normal?

A small change can come from a rate increase, a longer billing period, or an estimate catching up, none of which is a leak. Use the table above and your own history. If you're well above the benchmark for your household size, or the meter moves during the two-hour test, it's worth hunting further.
