For a cool-season lawn, the last cut should be no more than one notch (about half an inch) below your normal height, made only after the grass stops growing. For a lawn normally kept at 2.5 to 3 inches, that lands at 2 to 2.5 inches. Warm-season lawns stay in their normal range.

That answer sits between two pieces of advice you'll find online: "scalp it short for winter" and "leave it long to insulate the roots." K-State, NDSU, and Purdue all warn against leaving it long, and Purdue also warns against scalping. Extension answers differ on how low the last cut should go, and the checklist below shows where each one stands.

## The timing items

### 1. Keep mowing at your normal height while the grass still grows

Purdue University's turf program says to keep mowing as long as the grass continues to grow, which it says is normally into late October or early November. You can mow less often as growth slows, but don't stop early. University of Illinois Extension goes further: mow until the grass is completely dormant.

The reason is in the leaf. Purdue explains that photosynthesis stays high in fall, and the grass stores the resulting carbohydrates for winter and spring growth. Cutting off leaves early reduces what the grass can store.

### 2. Lower the deck only after top growth has essentially stopped

The "go lower" advice comes with a condition. An Ask Extension expert, answering a Michigan homeowner in November 2025, said to lower the mowing height only once the grass's top growth has essentially stopped for the season. Dropping the deck in early October, while the grass is still growing, means mowing short for weeks rather than once.

There's no date for this. Watch the lawn rather than the calendar: once top growth has essentially stopped, the next mow is your last.

### 3. Don't stop early and leave it tall

Stopping in September with a shaggy lawn is the opposite mistake. Purdue says unmowed grass encourages snow mold. North Dakota State University Extension says tall grass is more likely to be matted down and infected by snow mold.

Kansas State University Extension addresses the insulation idea directly. Some people believe taller grass protects the plant crowns over winter, but K-State says that causes problems when the blades fall over. The result is a matted layer that's ideal for winter diseases.

## The height items

### 4. Go down one notch at most

The Ask Extension answer is specific: lower the mowing height no more than one notch on your mower, typically about half an inch, for the final mow. It puts the ideal final height at around 2 to 2.5 inches. It says that height can reduce the risk of snow mold, discourage voles, and help the grass tolerate cold.

Two other extension services give a single number. Illinois says to mow at a final height of about two inches, and NDSU says the final mowing should be at two inches.

Purdue pushes back on going low. It warns against the urge to set the mower down and scalp the lawn for the final mowing. It also notes that older publications recommended mowing low in late fall and says to avoid that practice.

Only the Ask Extension answer sets a limit on how far to drop: one notch. Staying at the normal height is also within the advice: Iowa State says to mow at the recommended height throughout the fall.

### 5. Respect the one-third rule on the last cut too

Clemson Extension's rule is to remove no more than one-third of the grass height at any one mowing. The rule applies to every mowing, including the last one.

Here's what the rule means in practice. If your tall fescue has been at 3.5 inches and you drop straight to 2 inches, you remove 1.5 of 3.5 inches. That's 1.5 ÷ 3.5 = about 43%, well over a third. One notch, from 3.5 to 3 inches, removes 0.5 ÷ 3.5 = about 14%.

### 6. Use the table to find your last-cut height

The table below combines the Ask Extension answer's one-notch limit and its 2 to 2.5 inch target with Clemson's one-third rule. The last column is the tallest the grass can be when you make the final cut without removing more than a third. To find it, divide the final height by 2/3 (the same as multiplying by 1.5).

| Your normal height | One notch lower (−0.5 in) | Lands in the 2–2.5 in target? | Your final cut | Mow before grass reaches (final cut × 1.5) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 in | 1.5 in | No, it's below | Stay at 2 in; 1.5 in is under the lowest final height cited here | 3 in |
| 2.5 in | 2 in | Yes | 2 in | 3 in |
| 3 in | 2.5 in | Yes | 2.5 in | 3.75 in |
| 3.5 in | 3 in | No, it's above | 3 in; reaching 2.5 in would take two notches | 4.5 in |
| 4 in | 3.5 in | No, it's above | 3.5 in | 5.25 in |

The last two rows matter most. A lawn kept tall all summer can't reach 2 inches in one pass without breaking both the one-notch limit and the one-third rule. One notch lower is the most it should drop.

### 7. Warm-season lawns: stay in the normal range, toward the top

Warm-season grasses work the other way. K-State says they can be mowed at the higher end of their recommended heights in late summer and early fall, so they store more carbohydrates for winter. University of Georgia Extension adds that if you raised the mower half an inch during summer heat, fall is the time to lower it back to the normal height. Either way, the last cut stays inside the normal range, not below it.

| Grass | Normal height range | Last cut |
|---|---|---|
| Common Bermuda | 1–2 in (Clemson, K-State, UGA) | Your normal height; the higher end is an option (K-State) |
| Hybrid Bermuda | 0.5–1.5 in (Clemson) | Your normal height; not below the range |
| Zoysia | 1–2 in (Clemson, K-State, UGA) | Your normal height; the higher end is an option (K-State) |
| Centipede | 1.5–2 in (Clemson); UGA groups it at 1–2 in | Your normal height; not below the range |
| St. Augustine | 2.5–4 in (Clemson) | Your normal height; not below the range |

For cool-season grasses, K-State lists tall fescue at 2.5 to 3.5 inches and Kentucky bluegrass at 2 to 3 inches. Clemson's winter range for tall fescue is 2 to 3 inches. If you're not sure which group your lawn belongs to, [lawn care for people who don't care about lawns](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/07/lawn-care-for-people-who-dont-care.html) explains how to tell and lists the normal heights.

## The items around the mower

### 8. Clear or mulch the leaves on the same passes

What's lying on the grass matters as much as the mower setting. Iowa State lists raking up fallen leaves in fall as a snow mold measure. Purdue says leaves left covering the lawn cut into the photosynthesis that item 1 is protecting, and should be mowed off. Your last few mows can do both jobs. How many leaves a lawn can absorb is covered in [should you rake leaves or leave them over winter](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/09/should-you-rake-leaves-or-leave-them-on.html).

### 9. Skip the late fertilizer

NDSU says late fertilizer applications make turfgrass more susceptible to disease, and Iowa State lists avoiding excessive fall nitrogen as a snow mold measure. For cool-season lawns, that's a warning against overdoing it late, not against the usual fall feeding; the timing is in the lawn care guide linked above. Warm-season lawns get no fall feeding: UGA says fertilizing in September can delay dormancy long enough for cold injury to set in.

### 10. Finish the snow mold list

Iowa State's other prevention steps don't involve the mower:

- **Thatch:** keep it to 1/2 inch or less.
- **Snow piles:** spread out large piles, or use snow fencing on problem spots.
- **Next spring:** if matted, wet patches show up anyway, gently rake them to help them dry out.

Illinois also suggests clearing excess vegetation near the lawn, because it gives voles cover. The rest of the season's yard jobs, from hoses to gutters, are in the [fall yard cleanup checklist](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/07/fall-yard-cleanup-checklist-what.html).
