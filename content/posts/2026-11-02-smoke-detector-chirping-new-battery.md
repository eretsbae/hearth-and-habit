NFPA says a smoke alarm that keeps chirping after the battery has been replaced is at the end of its life and must be replaced. Before you buy a new one, rule out a loose battery, leftover charge, Hush mode, and the wrong alarm.

The National Fire Protection Association's tip sheet puts it plainly: a single chirp every 30 or 60 seconds means a low battery, and chirping that continues after the battery has been replaced means the alarm is at the end of its life. A reset helps only with leftover charge. First Alert says a reset does not fix end-of-life warnings or alarms that have reached the end of their usable service life.

So the job is to figure out which situation you're in. Start with the alarm's age.

## First check: the date on the back

Read the label on the back of the alarm. If it's hardwired, turn off its breaker before you take it off the mounting bracket; First Alert's hardwired steps start with cutting power at the breaker.

The U.S. Fire Administration (USFA) says smoke alarms need replacing **10 years from the manufacture date**, not 10 years from the day you put it up. An alarm that sat on a store shelf or in a closet before installation has already used part of its life. First Alert adds that if the label is missing or unreadable, you should replace the alarm immediately.

### Worked example: is your alarm already past its date?

Count from the manufacture month and year on the label to today (November 2026 here):

| Label says | Alarm type | Math | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Made March 2016 | Smoke alarm | March 2016 + 10 years = March 2026 | Past USFA's 10-year limit. Replace it. |
| Made December 2016 | Smoke alarm | December 2016 + 10 years = December 2026 | One to two months short of the limit, depending on the day. Work through the symptoms below, and plan the replacement anyway. |
| Made 2019 | Smoke alarm | 2019 + 10 years = 2029 | Under 10. Work through the symptoms below. |
| Powered up 2019 | Kidde CO alarm | 2019 + 7 years = 2026 | Kidde says its CO alarms start an end-of-life chirp seven years after first power-up. That chirp may be due now. |

To use your own numbers, add 10 years to your label's date. The 10-year limit comes from USFA. The 7-year CO clock is Kidde's figure for its own CO alarms; NFPA notes that CO end-of-life sounds vary by manufacturer, so check your manual.

## Match the chirp to the cause

If the alarm is under 10 years old, the pattern of the chirp is your best clue. This table is built from Kidde's published chirp meanings, NFPA's tip sheet, and First Alert's troubleshooting steps. Intervals vary by brand and model, so treat it as a guide and confirm with your manual.

| What you hear or see | Most likely cause | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Chirps every 30–40 seconds for about 7 minutes after you pressed the button, then stops | Hush mode on Kidde models with Hush. It uses the same 30–40 second interval as a low battery. | Let it run out. If the chirping keeps going past that, use the next row. |
| Chirps every 30–40 seconds (60 on some models) and keeps going after the new battery | Battery not making contact, drawer not closed, or leftover charge from the old battery | Reseat the battery, close the drawer, then do the reset below. |
| Same chirp returns right after a full reset with a fresh battery | End of life (NFPA) | Replace the alarm. First Alert says replacement may be the best solution at this point. |
| Kidde sealed smoke alarm (no battery door) chirps every 30 seconds | End of life on Kidde's sealed-battery models | Replace the whole alarm. There is no battery to change. |
| Kidde CO alarm chirps every 30 seconds without stopping, about 7 years after first power-up | Kidde's CO end-of-life warning | Replace it now. Kidde says the alarm no longer responds to carbon monoxide in this condition. Kidde's low-battery chirp on its CO alarms is once every 60 seconds instead. |
| Chirp comes between flashes of the red light | Malfunction on Kidde smoke alarms | Hold Test for at least 2 seconds. If it doesn't sound, Kidde says the unit likely has a defective battery or another failure. |
| Three loud beeps, repeating (four on a CO alarm) | Not a chirp: smoke or fire (three), carbon monoxide (four) | Get out, call 911, and stay out (NFPA). |

## If it's under 10 and still chirping: the reset

This is the cheap fix, and it only applies to the leftover-charge case.

1. **Check the battery itself.** First Alert says to confirm the positive and negative ends are lined up, the battery drawer is snapped completely shut, and the battery is the recommended type for that alarm.
2. **Drain the leftover power.** With the battery out, hold the Test button. First Alert gives 15 seconds on one support page and 15–20 seconds on another, so follow your manual if it differs.
3. **Reinstall and test.** Put the new battery back, close the drawer, and press Test.

If the chirp comes back right away after a full reset with a fresh battery, First Alert says replacement may be the best solution. At that point you've done what a reset can do.

## If it's hardwired: different steps

A hardwired alarm still chirps for a low battery because, as USFA notes, most have a backup battery. USFA says to replace that battery at least once a year.

The reset is also different. First Alert says to turn off power at the breaker, then on many hardwired models remove both the AC power and the backup battery before holding Test. First Alert also lists power surges, outages, and loose wiring as chirp causes on AC-powered units.

Replacing a hardwired unit is a different job from changing a battery. USFA says only qualified electricians should install hardwired smoke alarms. If you're weighing whether to do it yourself, [how to know if a repair is worth calling a pro](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/09/how-do-i-know-if-repair-is-actually.html) can help you decide.

## If it only chirps at night

This one has a physical cause. Kidde explains that a battery near the end of its life has internal resistance, and a cooler room raises that resistance. Most homes are coolest between 2 a.m. and 6 a.m., so a weak battery crosses the low-battery line then. The chirp may stop once the house warms up a few degrees.

Kidde says its alarms are designed for 40 to 100°F and doesn't recommend operating them at or outside those limits. It also says cold can weaken the battery and trigger a low-battery chirp.

If the chirp stopped by morning, the battery still needs replacing. If you put in a new battery and the night chirps continue, check the date on the back and the reset above.

## If you can't tell which alarm is chirping

In a house with several alarms, a chirp from a hallway can sound like it's coming from the bedroom. Before you decide a fresh battery "didn't work," make sure you changed the right alarm. Stand under each one in turn and wait through a couple of chirps. First Alert notes that beep and light patterns vary by model, so the manual for that specific alarm tells you what its light means.

## If the alarm is dusty

First Alert lists dust, insects, or debris inside the sensing chamber as a chirp cause. Follow your alarm's manual for cleaning it.

## What not to do

Don't leave the battery out "until morning." USFA says a smoke alarm with a dead or missing battery is the same as having no smoke alarm at all, and that disabling an alarm or removing the battery can be a deadly mistake. If an alarm has to come down, replace it the same day.

Don't silence and keep a Kidde CO alarm that is giving its CO end-of-life chirp. Kidde says it no longer responds to carbon monoxide in that state.

Sealed 10-year alarms run on a nonreplaceable lithium-ion battery, and USFA says to replace the entire alarm according to the manufacturer's instructions. Check those instructions before disposal; the EPA says lithium-ion batteries don't belong in household trash.

## Keep it from happening at 3 a.m. again

USFA's schedule is simple: test every alarm monthly, replace 9-volt and backup batteries at least once a year, and replace every alarm 10 years from its manufacture date. Write the replacement year on the alarm or in your home records the day you install it. A yearly battery swap fits naturally into [the home maintenance tasks most people forget](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/07/10-home-maintenance-tasks-most-people.html).
