A DIY home energy audit takes about 2 to 4 hours with a flashlight, a tape measure and a stick of incense, per Durham County's sustainability office. Most checklists stop at "look for drafts" and "check the insulation." This one goes further: for each room it lists what to inspect, what the typical loads in that room cost per year, and which fixes earn their money back fastest.

All dollar figures use 18 cents per kWh, the top of the roughly 17 to 18 cent US average residential price that [EIA reports](https://www.eia.gov/electricity/monthly/epm_table_grapher.php?t=epmt_5_6_a). State rates run from about 11 cents to over 40, so every figure below shows its arithmetic and you can swap in your own rate. The formula, as the Department of Energy puts it, is [watts × hours used ÷ 1,000 × price per kWh](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/estimating-appliance-and-home-electronic-energy-use).

## Before you start: bills, a 3 a.m. meter reading and a short tool list

**Pull 12 months of bills.** The [Durham County guide](https://dconc.gov/Engineering-and-Environmental1/Documents/Sustainability/DIY-guides/Home-Energy-Assessment.pdf) says to look for unusual spikes. A jump that lines up with a season may point at heating, cooling or a dehumidifier. A jump that doesn't may mean something new started running, and our [step-by-step guide to a high electric bill](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/09/why-is-my-electric-bill-so-high-step-by.html) walks through that case.

**Read your idle load.** If your utility shows smart-meter data by the hour, Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory suggests [checking a quiet hour such as 3 a.m.](https://standby.lbl.gov/meters-to-measure-standby-power), when the refrigerator compressor is off. What's left is your house's always-on load. LBNL warns that this reading can run high, because it's easy to overlook a light, a small fan or a pump that happens to be on.

For a sense of scale, NRDC analyzed smart-meter data from 70,000 northern California homes and found an [average always-on load of 164 watts](https://www.nrdc.org/sites/default/files/home-idle-load-IP.pdf). NRDC counted it over the 22 hours a day when nothing is actively in use:

- 164 W × 22 hours × 365 days ÷ 1,000 = 1,317 kWh, which NRDC rounds to about 1,300 kWh a year
- That was nearly 23% of household electricity use
- 1,300 kWh × $0.18 = $234 a year for always-on but inactive devices

Do the same math with your own 3 a.m. reading, and you'll know before you enter a single room whether idle load is worth chasing.

**Gather the tools.** Durham County's list is short: a checklist, a screwdriver for outlet covers, an N95 mask and gloves, a flashlight for attic corners and basement rim joists, an incense stick or tissue paper, a notebook, and a tape measure. ENERGY STAR's [DIY inspection guide](https://www.energystar.gov/saveathome/seal_insulate/identify-problems-you-want-fix/diy-checks-inspections) adds a voltage tester (for looking into outlet boxes), safety glasses and head protection for the attic. Pick a windy day, which makes drafts easier to find, and photograph problem areas as you go so you can rank them later. Both tips come from the Durham County guide.

**A plug-in watt meter is optional.** LBNL says these meters cost [under $50 online](https://standby.lbl.gov/frequently-asked-questions), lose accuracy below about 1 watt, and in most cases can't safely measure more than about 1,300 watts. They are fine for a TV cabinet or a desk. For anything that may draw more than that, such as a [space heater](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/10/how-much-does-it-cost-to-run-space.html), read the nameplate instead, keeping in mind that the nameplate shows the maximum draw.

This is the table most audit guides leave out. Two things stand out. First, NRDC's northern California estimate of always-on load (about 1,300 kWh) is a different kind of figure than the RECS averages, so don't rank it against them. Second, the biggest numbers live in the utility room and outdoors. These are national averages and your house will differ, so treat the table as a guide to where to look, not a bill forecast. At a 40 cent rate the same math gives 2,706 × $0.40 = $1,082.40 a year for electric water heating. For a broader ranking, see [which appliance uses the most electricity](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/08/which-appliance-in-your-house-is.html).

## Kitchen

**Refrigerators.** RECS puts the most-used refrigerator at 617 kWh a year, or 617 × $0.18 = $111.06. Set it to 35 to 38°F, the range ENERGY STAR gives on its [refrigerator page](https://www.energystar.gov/products/refrigerators). The same page says a refrigerator over 15 years old could be costing about $95 more per year to run. Write down its age if you can find it.

**Cooking, dishwasher, microwave.** These are small lines: $52.20, $20.16 and $19.26 a year at the RECS averages. They're not where the money goes.

**Small appliances with displays.** LBNL lists the features that point to standby draw: an external power supply, a remote control, a continuous display or a network connection. Count the kitchen devices that have one. You'll price them in the living-room step.

**Outlets on exterior walls.** If the kitchen has outlets on an outside wall, Durham County suggests adding foam gaskets behind the faceplates.

## Laundry room

The dryer averages 680 kWh, or $122.40 a year. That's the largest laundry line, and it scales with how many loads you dry. The washer's own electricity is small, $12.42 a year. RECS counts water heating as a separate end use.

If the laundry room has an exterior door or window, it gets the draft test described in the next section.

## Living room, media corner and home office: the always-on hunt

This is where NRDC found most of the idle load. Its 10-home field audit counted an average of 65 plugged-in devices per home, and 43 of them drew more than 1 watt while idle. NRDC's 10-home audit found consumer electronics (TVs, computers and connected gear) made up about half of always-on use. Heating, cooling, lighting and refrigeration together made up just 15%, and other miscellaneous loads (pumps, aquariums, outlets) the remaining 34%.

The arithmetic for any device that is always on:

- LBNL says 1 watt drawn constantly for a year uses about 9 kWh
- 9 kWh × $0.18 = $1.62 per year for every watt of always-on load
- A 10-watt idle device: 10 × $1.62 = $16.20 a year
- NRDC measured printers idling at 2 to 26 watts per home: 2 × $1.62 = $3.24 up to 26 × $1.62 = $42.12 a year

A $50 watt meter pays for itself in a year once it helps you cut 50 ÷ 1.62 = about 31 watts of always-on load.

What to do with what you find comes straight from LBNL. Unplug devices you rarely use, starting with the oldest, because older appliances tend to waste more. Put clusters of computer or video equipment on a switchable power strip so one switch cuts them all. When you replace something, look at its standby draw, since LBNL notes that new products can use up to 90% less standby power than the ones they replace. Our [phantom power guide](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/07/phantom-power-how-much-your-idle.html) covers which devices tend to be the worst offenders.

**TVs.** All TVs and related equipment average 632 kWh, or $113.76 a year. The most-used TV averages 210 kWh (210 × $0.18 = $37.80), and a third TV in a spare room still averages 82 kWh ($14.76).

**Lighting.** Durham County's guide says to count incandescent and CFL bulbs to upgrade to LEDs. RECS puts whole-house lighting at 654 kWh, or $117.72 a year.

**Windows and doors: the draft test.** Before you turn on any exhaust fan for the incense or tissue test, turn off combustion appliances such as a gas furnace and water heater. Turn them back on when you finish. Then close all windows, turn on the bathroom and kitchen exhaust fans, and pass a lit incense stick or a tissue slowly around each window and door frame. Where the smoke wavers or the tissue flutters, air is getting in. ENERGY STAR adds a paper test: close a door or window on a sheet of paper. If you can pull it out without tearing it, the seal leaks. Photograph each leak. [Caulking 101](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/07/caulking-101-where-to-caulk-what-to-buy.html) covers what to seal it with.

**Ceiling fans** average 226 kWh, or $40.68 a year.

## Bathrooms

Showering is nearly 17% of indoor water use, according to EPA WaterSense. Standard showerheads flow at 2.5 gallons per minute, while [WaterSense models](https://www.epa.gov/watersense/showerheads) are capped at 2.0. EPA estimates the average family saves 2,700 gallons and more than 330 kWh a year by switching, because less water needs heating. That's 330 × $0.18 = $59.40 a year or more if your water heater is electric. Check the flow rate stamped on your showerhead.

While you're there, the bathroom exhaust fan does double duty in the draft test above.

## Utility room and basement: water heater, furnace, ducts, dehumidifier

For a home with electric water heating, this room holds the largest line in the cost table.

**Water heater temperature.** Durham County recommends lowering the setting to 120°F (49°C) to cut standby losses. Our guide on [whether 120°F saves money](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/10/does-setting-water-heater-to-120f.html) covers what to check before you turn the dial.

**Feel the tank.** Advanced Energy, based in Raleigh, North Carolina, says electric tanks that feel hot on the outside, and electric tanks in spaces that get very cold, gain the most from an [insulating blanket](https://www.advancedenergy.org/news/electric-blankets-for-water-heaters). Newer tanks come with more factory insulation. Advanced Energy also suggests insulating the first few feet of hot and cold pipe at the tank. If the heater is older than about 15 years, they suggest you may do better replacing it with a more efficient model. That's where the heat pump math in the payback table comes in.

**Furnace and heat pump.** Durham County's checklist asks three things: is the filter clean, are the duct joints sealed, and is the outdoor unit clear of debris? ENERGY STAR's [maintenance checklist](https://www.energystar.gov/saveathome/heating-cooling/maintenance-checklist) says to inspect, clean or change filters monthly, and to book a contractor check-up each spring for cooling and each fall for heating. Our [furnace filter guide](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/07/furnace-filter-basics-how-often-to.html) covers sizes and replacement intervals.

**Ducts.** ENERGY STAR says a typical house loses about 20 to 30 percent of the air moving through its [duct system](https://www.energystar.gov/saveathome/heating-cooling/duct-sealing) to leaks, holes and poor connections. Warning signs include rooms that are hard to heat or cool, stuffy rooms, ducts running through an attic, crawlspace or garage, and kinked flexible duct. Seal joints you can reach with mastic or metal tape, never standard duct tape, and insulate ducts in unconditioned spaces. If one room never keeps up, [this troubleshooting guide](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/09/why-is-one-room-in-my-house-always.html) goes further.

**Rim joists.** Shine the flashlight along the top of the basement wall where the floor framing sits. Durham County singles out this spot.

**Dehumidifier.** At 999 kWh, or $179.82 a year, a dehumidifier averages more than the RECS figure for all of a household's refrigerators combined (839 kWh). Put a humidity reading next to it. EPA's [mold guidance](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home) is to keep indoor humidity below 60%, ideally 30 to 50%.

**Thermostat.** The Department of Energy says turning the thermostat back 7 to 10°F for 8 hours a day can save [up to about 10% a year](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/programmable-thermostats) on heating and cooling. Check what yours is actually set to. [Thermostat settings for every season](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/07/the-right-thermostat-settings-for-every.html) has a schedule to copy.

## Attic

**Safety first.** ENERGY STAR's rules are specific. Walk only on joists or truss chords, never on drywall or insulation. Watch for nails. Wear head protection, safety glasses, gloves and a dust mask. If the insulation is vermiculite, don't disturb it. It may contain asbestos and should be tested by an approved lab first. If your insulation sits against the roof deck, as in a cathedral ceiling, ENERGY STAR says to hire an energy auditor or insulation contractor.

**Hatch.** Shine a light at the attic hatch and look for light showing around its edges, as the Durham County guide describes.

**The joist test.** ENERGY STAR says that if the insulation is level with or below the top of the floor joists, you probably need more. If you can't see the joists at all, you probably have enough, and adding more may not be cost-effective. Look for low spots too, since insulation should be evenly spread.

**Measure, then convert.** Push the tape measure down to the ceiling drywall in a few spots and note the depth and the material. ENERGY STAR gives the R-value per inch for each type, so you can turn a depth into an R-value, and a target into a depth:

| Material | R per inch | Inches for R-38 (38 ÷ R per inch) | Inches for R-49 (49 ÷ R per inch) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Loose-fill fiberglass | 2.5 | 15.2 | 19.6 |
| Rock wool | 2.8 | 13.6 | 17.5 |
| Fiberglass batts | 3.2 | 11.9 | 15.3 |
| Cellulose | 3.7 | 10.3 | 13.2 |

A worked example: 6 inches of loose-fill fiberglass is 6 × 2.5 = R-15. Reaching R-49 takes 49 − 15 = R-34 more, which is 34 ÷ 2.5 = 13.6 more inches of the same material. ENERGY STAR's quicker [attic check](https://energystar.gov/products/ask-the-experts/how-check-your-homes-attic-insulation-level) multiplies depth by 3. By that method the South needs at least R-38 (about 13 to 14 inches) and the North at least R-49 (about 16 to 18 inches). The table shows why the material matters, since loose fiberglass needs more depth than that estimate.

**Which target?** ENERGY STAR recommends, for an uninsulated attic, R30 to R49 in climate zone 1, R30 to R60 in zones 2 and 3, R38 to R60 in zone 4, and R49 to R60 in zones 5 through 8. The Durham County guide cites R-49 to R-60 as the cost-optimal target from DOE and ENERGY STAR. ENERGY STAR suggests adding insulation if you're 4 to 6 inches or more below the recommended level.

## Exterior walls, doors and the garage

**Wall insulation.** ENERGY STAR's method: turn off the power to an outlet on an exterior wall, confirm it's dead with a voltage tester, remove the cover and look beside the box. Check outlets on every floor and in both old and new parts of the house. Insulation in one wall doesn't mean it's in all of them, and exterior walls should be completely filled.

**Exterior doors.** Durham County says to look for daylight around the edges. ENERGY STAR suggests a night version: a partner shines a flashlight around the frame from outside while you watch for light from inside.

**Second refrigerator or freezer.** At the RECS averages, a second refrigerator costs 494 × $0.18 = $88.92 a year and a separate freezer 570 × $0.18 = $102.60. If the second refrigerator holds a few drinks, it's the easiest money in the house. If you need one year-round, ENERGY STAR's advice is to replace an old unit with a certified model and recycle the old one. A freezer that frosts up fast is worth checking too, and our [freezer frost guide](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/10/why-does-my-freezer-build-up-frost-so.html) covers the causes.

**Outside.** Keep the AC condenser clear of debris (Durham County). If you have a pool pump ($429.12 a year at the RECS average) or a hot tub ($353.34 a year for heater plus pump), they belong near the top of your list. EV charging averages $425.34 a year. That isn't waste, but it explains a bill that jumped the month the car arrived.

## Which fixes pay back first

Payback in months is the fix cost divided by the yearly savings, times 12. Costs and savings rates come from the cited sources, and the savings are priced at 18 cents. Where no source gives a price, the table shows the most you could spend and still break even in the stated time.

| Fix | Upfront cost | Yearly savings at $0.18/kWh | Payback |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unplug a second refrigerator you don't need | $0 | 494 kWh × $0.18 = $88.92 | Immediate |
| Thermostat setback, programmable thermostat already installed | $0 | Up to about 10% of heating and cooling. For an all-electric home at the RECS averages: ($447.12 + $417.24) × 10% = up to about $86 | Immediate (if you set it back as described) |
| Water heater blanket (electric tank) | About $20 (Advanced Energy, 2013 article) | 5% to 9% of $487.08 (DOE range via Advanced Energy) = $24.35 to $43.84 | $20 ÷ $43.84 × 12 = 5.5 months, up to $20 ÷ $24.35 × 12 = 9.9 months |
| Watt meter, then unplug or strip the idle loads it finds | Under $50 (LBNL) | $1.62 per watt removed | 12 months if it helps cut 31 watts |
| WaterSense showerhead (electric water heater) | Not cited | 330 kWh × $0.18 = $59.40 or more | Within 12 months if it costs under $59.40 |
| Air sealing plus attic insulation | Varies by house | Up to 10% of energy bills (ENERGY STAR). On electricity alone at EIA's 10,500 kWh average: 10,500 × $0.18 = $1,890, × 10% = $189 | 24 months on a job costing up to $189 × 2 = $378 |
| Heat pump water heater, household of 2 | $1,503 extra over a standard tank | 1,880 kWh × $0.18 = $338.40 | $1,503 ÷ $338.40 = 4.44 years, about 53 months |
| Heat pump water heater, household of 3 | $1,503 extra | 2,820 kWh × $0.18 = $507.60 | $1,503 ÷ $507.60 = 2.96 years, about 36 months |
| Heat pump water heater, household of 4 | $1,503 extra | 3,760 kWh × $0.18 = $676.80 | $1,503 ÷ $676.80 = 2.22 years, about 27 months |

A few notes on the table:

- **The blanket.** Advanced Energy calls the popular "up to $200 a year" claim unrealistic. Insulating the tank cuts standby losses by 25 to 45%, but standby is only part of water heating energy, which is why the overall range is 5 to 9%.
- **The heat pump water heater.** The kWh savings, the $1,503 incremental cost and the 13-year lifespan come from [ENERGY STAR's savings table](https://www.energystar.gov/products/heat_pump_water_heaters/benefits-savings). ENERGY STAR priced those savings at 14.6 cents and got paybacks of 5.5, 3.7 and 2.7 years. At 18 cents, every payback shortens. ENERGY STAR's household-size savings are larger than the 2,706 kWh RECS average for electric water heating, so check your own usage before counting on the 3- and 4-person rows.
- **Air sealing and insulation.** The $1,890 baseline is from EIA's [average of about 10,500 kWh a year](https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/use-of-energy/electricity-use-in-homes.php) per household. If you heat with gas, apply the same 10% to your gas bill too.

The zero-cost rows come first. The blanket pays back inside a year at the DOE range Advanced Energy cites, and a watt meter does only if it helps you remove about 31 watts. The heat pump water heater pays back fastest in larger households.

## Printable room-by-room checklist

Built from the Durham County Sustainability Office's assessment guide and ENERGY STAR's DIY inspection guide. Print it, carry it and a pencil, and note each finding with a photo.

**Before you start**

- 12 months of bills, with spikes circled
- 3 a.m. smart-meter reading, if available: ___ watts × 22 × 365 ÷ 1,000 × your rate (NRDC's 22-hour assumption)
- Flashlight, tape measure, screwdriver, voltage tester, incense or tissue, notebook
- N95 mask, gloves, safety glasses, head protection
- A windy day, with combustion appliances off before any fan-assisted draft test
- Smoke and CO alarms tested (NFPA says to test both monthly)

**Kitchen**

- Refrigerator set to 35 to 38°F; age noted
- Devices with displays, remotes or power bricks counted
- Foam gaskets behind outlet covers on exterior walls

**Laundry**

- Draft test at any exterior door or window

**Living areas and office**

- Always-on devices listed (watt meter if you have one)
- Clusters of electronics on a switchable power strip
- Incandescent and CFL bulbs counted
- Incense or tissue test at every window and door frame
- Paper test on doors and windows that seem loose

**Bathrooms**

- Showerhead flow rate checked (2.5 gpm standard, 2.0 or less WaterSense)

**Utility room and basement**

- Water heater at 120°F; tank warm to the touch?
- First few feet of water heater pipe insulated
- Furnace or heat pump filter clean
- Duct joints sealed with mastic or metal tape
- Rim joists checked with the flashlight
- Dehumidifier needed? Humidity below 60%, ideally 30 to 50%
- Thermostat schedule checked

**Attic**

- Vermiculite? Stop and test before touching
- Light visible around the hatch?
- Insulation above or below the joists?
- Depth in three spots: ___ inches × R per inch = R-___
- Low spots or bare areas

**Exterior walls, doors, garage, outside**

- Insulation beside outlet boxes on every floor (power off, tester confirms)
- Daylight around exterior doors
- Second refrigerator or freezer: needed year-round?
- AC condenser clear of debris
- Pool pump, hot tub and EV charging noted as line items

**After the walk-through**

- Rank each finding by upfront cost, expected savings and difficulty (Durham County's three criteria)

A DIY walk-through finds the obvious problems. ENERGY STAR still recommends a professional energy audit for a thorough look at the whole house, and specifically for homes with cathedral ceilings.
