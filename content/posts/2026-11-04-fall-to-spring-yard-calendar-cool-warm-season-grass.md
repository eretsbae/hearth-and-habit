Cool-season lawns get their most important care in fall, and warm-season lawns get theirs in late spring. So the same month often calls for opposite jobs. This calendar runs September through May for both grass types, and it ties each task to the soil or air temperature that university extension services use to time it.

Most lawn calendars stop at the month names. Months shift with your latitude, so this guide also gives you the triggers behind them: the soil temperature that ends fescue seeding, the one that starts crabgrass germination, and the night temperature that means a bermuda lawn is ready for fertilizer. Every threshold below names the extension service that published it.

## First, figure out which calendar is yours

Lawn grasses split into two groups that grow at different times of year:

- Cool-season grasses include tall fescue, Kentucky bluegrass, and perennial ryegrass. They are the common lawns of the North and grow most in spring and fall.
- Warm-season grasses include bermuda, St. Augustine, zoysia, and centipede. They are the common lawns of the South, go brown and dormant in winter, and green up in late spring.

Clemson's Home & Garden Information Center sums up the practical difference: warm-season lawns are fertilized from late-spring green-up through summer and not in fall, while cool-season lawns get their main feeding in fall.

If you're not sure which you have, ask your county extension office before following either column below.

The middle of the country, often called the transition zone, grows both kinds. Oklahoma State University publishes a cool-season calendar for tall fescue and bluegrass there, and Clemson publishes warm-season calendars for South Carolina. Both appear below, and the month-to-month differences between them are a big reason to watch temperatures instead of dates.

## The triggers that beat the calendar

These are the thresholds behind the monthly tasks. Two details matter here. A soil temperature means a thermometer pushed into the soil to the depth given, not the air reading on your phone. And several triggers use the overnight low, not the daytime high.

| Task | Grass | Trigger | How it's measured | Who says so |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Start fall seeding | Cool-season | Nights in the mid-60s °F | Overnight air temperature | NC State Extension |
| Stop fall seeding | Tall fescue, Kentucky bluegrass | Soil drops to 50°F | Soil temperature | Rutgers |
| Spring seeding can germinate | Cool-season | Soil consistently above 50°F, which usually takes air temperatures of 60°F or more | Soil, with air as a proxy | Rutgers |
| Crabgrass preventer must be down | Northern cool-season lawns | Before soil averages 55°F for three to five days | Soil at 2 inches deep | NDSU Extension |
| Most crabgrass germinates | Northern cool-season lawns | Soil at 60–70°F | Soil | NDSU Extension |
| Winter-weed pre-emergent | Warm-season | Before nights drop below 60°F | Overnight air temperature | UGA Extension |
| Raise the mower slightly | Bermuda | Nights fall below 70°F | Overnight air temperature | Clemson HGIC |
| Fall broadleaf weed spray | Cool-season | A day of at least 50°F, with no rain or irrigation for 24 hours after | Daytime air temperature | K-State |
| Late-fall nitrogen | Cool-season | Near or after the last mowing, while the lawn is still green | Look at the lawn | Purdue |
| Green-up begins | Warm-season | Soil reaches 65°F | Soil at 4 inches deep | Clemson HGIC |
| First nitrogen of the year | Warm-season | After full green-up, with nights around 70°F | Lawn color plus overnight air temperature | Clemson HGIC |
| Water a dormant lawn | Bermuda | No measurable rain for 3–4 weeks, especially before a forecast in the low 20s or colder | Rain gauge and forecast | Clemson HGIC |

![Soil temperature scale showing 50°F fescue seeding cutoff, 55°F crabgrass preventer deadline, 60 to 70°F crabgrass germination, and 65°F warm-season green-up](/images/fall-to-spring-yard-calendar-cool-warm-season-grass-inline.svg)

Some notes on reading the table:

- The 50°F and 55°F thresholds are close. Rutgers (New Jersey) gives 50°F soil as the spring germination minimum for cool-season seed, and NDSU (North Dakota) gives 55°F at 2 inches as the crabgrass preventer deadline. They come from different states and studies, but the two numbers are only 5 degrees apart.
- Depth matters. NDSU measures at 2 inches; Clemson's green-up figure is at 4 inches. Take each reading at the depth its source names.
- Clemson's bermuda program uses fixed dates for pre-emergent. It schedules them by region of South Carolina rather than by soil temperature. NDSU's 55°F trigger is written for northern cool-season lawns, so don't apply it to bermuda; for warm-season lawns outside South Carolina, use your own state extension's dates.

### Fall: the cool-season lawn's big season

For a fescue or bluegrass lawn, September and October matter more than the rest of the year combined. Seeding, the most important feeding, and weed control all happen in the same few weeks, and each has its own cutoff.

Seeding has a start trigger and a stop trigger. NC State's Grady Miller suggests waiting until nights are in the mid-60s. At the other end, Rutgers found that tall fescue and bluegrass don't form a seedling turf once soil drops to 50°F. NC State adds that fall-seeded grass should get no herbicide until it has thickened and tillered (sent up side shoots), so seeding usually means skipping the fall weed spray on those patches. The full late-season cutoff discussion is in [how late in the fall you can still seed grass and have it take](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/07/how-late-can-you-seed-grass-in-fall-and.html).

Weeds are the other fall job. K-State's November advice is to spray broadleaf weeds even when they are small, on a day of at least 50°F. Rain or irrigation within 24 hours reduces how well it works. Follow the product label for rates.

Leaves and mowing round out the season. Keep mowing as long as the grass grows; [the bare-minimum lawn guide](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/07/lawn-care-for-people-who-dont-care.html) has the mowing height for each grass type. Leaves that cover the grass need to be mulched or moved, and [whether to rake leaves or leave them](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/09/should-you-rake-leaves-or-leave-them-on.html) explains where the line falls.

### Fall: why a warm-season lawn gets no "winterizer"

Many fall fertilizer bags are sold as "winterizer." For a bermuda lawn, Clemson's program is plain: no nitrogen from September through December. UGA Extension in Forsyth County explains why. Fertilizing in September can delay dormancy long enough for the grass to suffer cold injury, and fall nitrogen sets lawns up for spring dead spot, a lawn disease.

What a warm-season lawn may get in fall is potassium, and only if a soil test shows it's low. Clemson specifies 1 lb of potash (K₂O) per 1,000 sq ft, applied 4 to 6 weeks before the first expected frost. UGA notes that a pre-emergent herbicide containing potassium (it gives 0-0-7 as an example grade) can improve bermuda's winter hardiness. UGA doesn't say such a product replaces a soil-test-based potash rate, so let the soil test set the potash amount. Follow the product label and don't exceed labeled rates.

The warm-season fall job that does matter is the pre-emergent for winter weeds. UGA's timing is September, before nights drop below 60°F, because that's when soil cools enough for winter weed seeds to sprout. Clemson adds a second application 8 to 10 weeks later and says to water granular products in. Follow the product label and don't exceed labeled rates.

### Winter: mostly rest, with three exceptions

Dormant lawns need little, but three jobs don't wait for spring.

1. Water a dormant warm-season lawn in a dry spell. Clemson's bermuda program says to water during dormancy if there's been no measurable rain for 3 to 4 weeks, especially before days forecast in the low 20s or colder.
2. Keep salt off the edges. Grass along a sidewalk or driveway that dies every winter is often salt damage rather than cold. [Why grass along the sidewalk dies every winter](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/09/why-does-grass-along-sidewalk-die-every.html) covers how to use less salt and repair the strip in spring.
3. Finish the outdoor plumbing before the first hard freeze. Hoses off, faucets drained. [Shutting off the outdoor faucet from inside](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/10/should-you-shut-off-outdoor-faucet-from.html) shows where the valve usually is.

### Spring: crabgrass preventer or seed, not both

One spring mistake to avoid is putting crabgrass preventer on a lawn that was just seeded. NDSU's warning is direct: do not apply a standard crabgrass preventer to newly seeded or overseeded lawns. A pre-emergent works by stopping seeds from germinating, and it can't tell crabgrass seed from grass seed. Oklahoma State's calendar repeats the warning and names siduron (sold as Tupersan) as the only preemergent that can be used at seeding time on cool-season turf.

That leaves cool-season owners a choice each spring:

- Choose prevention. Put preventer down before soil averages 55°F at 2 inches, water it in with about half an inch of water (NDSU), and save seeding for fall. NDSU notes that most pre-emergents won't control crabgrass once it has sprouted.
- Choose seeding. Oklahoma State ranks spring as the second-choice season for seeding. Rutgers says cool-season seed won't germinate until soil stays above 50°F. NC State adds that spring-seeded tall fescue is more prone to drought, heat, disease, and weeds. If you seed anyway, skip the standard preventer or use a siduron product on those areas.

On a warm-season lawn, the spring trap is fertilizing too early. Clemson notes that new shoots don't appear until soil reaches 65°F at a 4-inch depth. Fertilizer applied before then pushes top growth at the expense of roots that are still regrowing, and can set the lawn up for dieback from a late frost. Clemson's guidance is to wait until nights are around 70°F; most warm-season grasses are actively growing by early to mid-May.

## How many times does a cool-season lawn need fertilizer?

Fewer than most bag programs suggest. Iowa State Extension offers three levels of feeding, and the lowest is a single application:

| Program | Mid-September | Late October / early November | Spring (April or May) | Feedings a year |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Minimal | — | Yes | — | 1 |
| Moderate | Yes | Yes | — | 2 |
| High-quality | Yes | Yes | Yes | 3 |

*Built from Iowa State Extension's fertilizer FAQ. All three levels skip June, July, and August.*

The one feeding every program keeps is the late one. Purdue's Aaron Patton puts it near or after the last mowing, while the lawn is still green, generally in the first few weeks of November. He recommends 0.5 to 1.0 lb of nitrogen per 1,000 sq ft from a quick-release (soluble) source, such as urea or ammonium sulfate, because the grass has to take the nitrogen up before winter. Purdue says most of the payoff shows up the following spring: earlier green-up, a denser lawn, and better tolerance of spring diseases. Check your state or local fertilizer rules before this step; some limit late-fall applications.

If the lawn is a low-priority patch you only want to look decent, that single November feeding pairs well with the minimum routine in [lawn care for people who don't care about lawns](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/07/lawn-care-for-people-who-dont-care.html).

## Working out the dates and amounts yourself

Extension calendars give intervals and rates. Most homeowners need the actual dates and pounds, so here is the arithmetic.

### When the second pre-emergent is due

Clemson's bermuda program calls for a second pre-emergent 8 to 10 weeks (56 to 70 days) after each first application. Taking the 15th as "mid-month," and using the 2026–27 season, the windows work out like this:

| First application (Clemson) | + 8 weeks | + 10 weeks | Second application window |
|---|---|---|---|
| Feb 15 (coastal and central SC) | Apr 12 | Apr 26 | Mid to late April |
| Mar 15 (Piedmont and mountains) | May 10 | May 24 | Mid to late May |
| Sept 1 (early in Clemson's September window) | Oct 27 | Nov 10 | Late October to early November |
| Sept 30 (late in the window) | Nov 25 | Dec 9 | Late November to early December |

Put your own first-application date on a calendar and count forward 56 and 70 days. The spring second dose can land in your green-up weeks; Clemson's schedule puts it there. Clemson's no-weed-killer rule during green-up is about post-emergent products.

### How much product to spread

Clemson gives a shortcut for converting a nitrogen rate into pounds of product. The first number on a fertilizer bag is its nitrogen percentage.

> Pounds of product per 1,000 sq ft = (pounds of nitrogen wanted × 100) ÷ first number on the bag

That's Clemson's rule written out: for ½ lb of nitrogen, divide 50 by the first number; for 1 lb, divide 100 by it. With the 15-0-15 product Clemson names for summer bermuda feedings, on a lawn measuring 5,000 sq ft:

> ½ lb N: 50 ÷ 15 = 3.33 lb per 1,000 sq ft × 5 = 16.7 lb of product
>
> 1 lb N: 100 ÷ 15 = 6.67 lb per 1,000 sq ft × 5 = 33.3 lb of product

The same formula covers Purdue's November rate for cool-season lawns (0.5 to 1.0 lb of nitrogen per 1,000 sq ft). Swap in the first number on your bag and your lawn's square footage.

The rule also checks Clemson's potash figures. For 1 lb of K₂O per 1,000 sq ft, 100 ÷ 60 = 1.67 lb of 0-0-60, and Clemson lists 1.6 lb, and 100 ÷ 50 = 2 lb of 0-0-50. For potash, use the third number on the bag instead of the first.

## Printable checklist

### Cool-season lawn (fescue, bluegrass, ryegrass)

*Fall*
- Seed or overseed when nights reach the mid-60s; finish before soil hits 50°F.
- Fertilize around Labor Day or mid-September.
- Spray broadleaf weeds in fall on a day of at least 50°F, but skip newly seeded areas.
- Keep mowing until growth stops, then make the last cut.
- Late-fall nitrogen near or after the last mowing (0.5–1.0 lb N per 1,000 sq ft).
- Mulch or move leaves that cover the grass.

*Winter*
- Keep de-icing salt and plowed snow off the turf edges.
- Outdoor faucets shut off and drained.

*Spring*
- Crabgrass preventer down before soil averages 55°F at 2 inches; water it in. Not on new seed.
- Optional spring feeding (April or May), then none in June through August.
- Grub prevention only where grubs have been a repeat problem.

### Warm-season lawn (bermuda example)

*Fall*
- No nitrogen from September on.
- Winter-weed pre-emergent in September, before nights drop below 60°F; second dose 8–10 weeks later.
- Raise the mower slightly once nights fall below 70°F.
- Potash 4–6 weeks before first frost, only if a soil test shows low potassium.

*Winter*
- Water if there's been no measurable rain for 3–4 weeks, especially before a forecast in the low 20s or colder.
- No nitrogen (coastal lawns excepted when no frost is predicted).

*Spring*
- Pre-emergent mid-February (coastal and central SC) or mid-March (Piedmont and mountains); second dose 8–10 weeks later.
- Mow at about 1 inch just before green-up; dethatch only if thatch is over half an inch thick.
- No post-emergent weed killer during green-up.
- First nitrogen after full green-up, with nights around 70°F: ½–1 lb N per 1,000 sq ft.
