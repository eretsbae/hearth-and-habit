In a refrigerator holding 40°F or below, cooked leftovers keep 3 to 4 days, raw ground meat and poultry 1 to 2 days, raw steaks, chops and roasts 3 to 5 days, fresh fish 1 to 3 days, opened lunch meat 3 to 5 days, and eggs in the shell 3 to 5 weeks. In a freezer at 0°F, food stays safe indefinitely, so the months you see on freezer charts are limits for taste and texture, not for safety. Perishable food that sits out more than 2 hours (1 hour above 90°F) goes in the trash no matter what the chart says.

Those are the federal numbers most storage charts reproduce. The chart below adds dairy figures from Oregon State University Extension, and the sections after it cover what a single chart leaves out: the cold fridge it assumes, why charts disagree, how to count days, and what to keep after the power goes out.

For the shorter version of the core rules (the 2-hour rule, cooling, reheating, thawing), the [leftovers, thawing, and fridge storage hub](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/09/leftovers-thawing-and-fridge-storage.html) has them on one page. This guide is the reference chart that sits behind it.

## The consolidated fridge and freezer chart

Fridge times assume 40°F or below. Freezer times assume 0°F and are quality limits; food kept frozen at 0°F stays safe past them, though it gets drier and blander. "Federal chart" means the FoodSafety.gov cold food storage chart, read from the Virginia Department of Health's copy.

### Raw meat and poultry

| Food | Fridge (40°F or below) | Freezer (0°F, quality) | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ground beef, turkey, chicken, pork, lamb, and mixes | 1–2 days | 3–4 months | Federal chart |
| Whole chicken or turkey | 1–2 days | 1 year | Federal chart |
| Chicken or turkey pieces | 1–2 days | 9 months | Federal chart |
| Steaks, chops, roasts (beef, veal, lamb, pork) | 3–5 days | 4–12 months | Federal chart |
| Sausage, raw | 1–2 days | 1–2 months | Federal chart |
| Fresh ham, uncured, uncooked | 3–5 days | 6 months | Federal chart |

### Bacon, hot dogs, lunch meat, and ham

| Food | Fridge (40°F or below) | Freezer (0°F, quality) | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hot dogs, unopened | 2 weeks | 1–2 months | Federal chart |
| Hot dogs, opened | 1 week | 1–2 months | Federal chart |
| Lunch meat, unopened | 2 weeks | 1–2 months | Federal chart |
| Lunch meat, opened or deli-sliced | 3–5 days | 1–2 months | Federal chart |
| Bacon | 1 week | 1 month | Federal chart |
| Sausage, fully cooked | 1 week | 1–2 months | Federal chart |
| Ham, cooked, store-wrapped, whole | 1 week | 1–2 months | Federal chart |
| Ham, cooked, slices, half, or spiral-cut | 3–5 days | 1–2 months | Federal chart |
| Ham, fully cooked, vacuum-sealed, unopened | 2 weeks or the "use by" date | 1–2 months | Federal chart |

### Cooked food and leftovers

| Food | Fridge (40°F or below) | Freezer (0°F, quality) | Source |
|---|---|---|---|

| Leftover cooked meat or poultry | 3–4 days | 2–6 months | Federal chart |

| Chicken nuggets or patties | 3–4 days | 1–3 months | Federal chart |

Read across the chart and the fridge column falls into a few tiers. Raw ground meat, raw poultry, raw sausage, fin fish, and gravy or broth are 1-to-2-day foods (fish runs to 3). Cooked leftovers, soups, deli salads, and pizza are 3-to-4-day foods. Whole cuts of raw meat and opened lunch meat get 3 to 5 days. Cured and cooked meats in their packaging run a week or two, and hard cheese, butter, and shell eggs are measured in weeks or months. If you lose the chart, knowing which tier a food sits in gets you a rough answer; check the row for exceptions.

Milk and yogurt are missing on purpose: none of the official sources used here gives a fridge-day figure for them, so this guide doesn't make one up. Check the package label, keep them cold, and treat them as perishable during an outage, as the table further down does.

Produce follows different rules, mostly about humidity and ethylene; for leafy greens, see [why greens wilt two days after you buy them](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/08/why-do-my-greens-wilt-two-days-after-i.html).

## The chart only works if the fridge is actually at 40°F

Every row above carries the same hidden condition: a refrigerator at 40°F or below, and a freezer at 0°F. Oregon State's chart says so in a footnote, and the federal chart prints it in the column headers. Few homes check.

Ohio State University's food-safety extension site reports that in observed homes, 43% of refrigerators were too warm (above 41°F), and only 4 to 9% of home fridges had a thermometer in them. It adds that predicted listeriosis cases would drop by more than 70% if every home fridge stayed at 40°F or below. FDA adds that few refrigerator controls show the actual temperature; the dial is a setting, not a reading.

Listeria matters here because, as FDA notes, it can grow at refrigerator temperatures. A fridge running above 40°F puts food outside the conditions the chart was written for. FDA's fix is simple: one appliance thermometer in the fridge, another in the freezer, checked often, and shelves loose enough for cold air to move.

Smell won't catch the problem either. The bacteria that make people sick generally don't change how food looks, smells, or tastes, which is why every rule on this page is about time and temperature. (If the fridge smells off with nothing spoiled in sight, the [fridge smell troubleshooting guide](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/08/why-does-my-fridge-smell-but-i-cant.html) covers drip pans and drains.)

## Why the charts disagree, and which number to use

Put three storage charts side by side and the numbers won't all match. Most of the gaps come from two things. First, freezer times measure quality. Oregon State University Extension's frozen food storage guide says plainly that its recommendations "are for quality not safety," and FDA says food kept at 0°F is safe indefinitely while quality drops the longer it stays frozen. So the freezer numbers vary with each author's sense of when texture and flavor fade:

| Food | Federal chart | Oregon State frozen food guide | Minnesota Dept. of Health |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pork chops, frozen | 4–12 months (all chops) | 4 months | Pork 4–8 months |
| Beef steaks and roasts, frozen | 4–12 months | 12 months | Beef 6–12 months |
| Whole chicken or turkey, frozen | 1 year | 12 months | Fresh poultry 6–9 months |
| Soups and stews, fridge | 3–4 days | — | 3–5 days |

So a chicken kept at 0°F past its year is a quality question, not a safety one.

The fridge rows are different, because those are safety limits. Minnesota's chart was last revised in 2007 and gives soups, stews, and deli salads 3 to 5 days; the federal chart (copy reviewed September 2021) gives 3 to 4. When two official charts disagree on a fridge time, use the shorter one. The cost of being wrong in that direction is a container of soup; the cost in the other direction is a stomach bug.

Two more notes on the freezer itself. Oregon State's guide points out that the small freezer located on top of a refrigerator is "not made for long term storage," so the longest freezer times fit a stand-alone freezer or a well-sealed one better than an over-the-fridge compartment. And FDA calls freezer burn "a food-quality issue, not a food safety issue": it happens when packaging isn't airtight. If your freezer frosts up fast, the causes are covered in [why a freezer builds up frost](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/10/why-does-my-freezer-build-up-frost-so.html).

## The two clocks: unopened and opened

Packaged meats have two storage times, and the one that matters is usually the shorter, opened one. On the federal and Oregon State charts:

- Lunch meat goes from 2 weeks unopened to 3 to 5 days once the package is opened or the meat is sliced at the deli counter.
- Hot dogs go from 2 weeks unopened to 1 week opened.
- Hard cheese goes from 6 months unopened to 3 to 4 weeks opened, per Oregon State.

The printed date doesn't settle it either. FDA says a "use by" date is a recommendation for best quality and is "not a food safety date," except on infant formula and some baby foods, which should be used by the date. Minnesota's health department likewise says product dates aren't a reliable guide. Write the opening date on the package with a marker and count from there.

CDC adds a layer for people at higher risk from Listeria: pregnant people, newborns, adults 65 and older, and anyone with a weakened immune system. For them, CDC says to eat deli meat and hot dogs only when heated to 165°F or until steaming hot, to avoid refrigerated smoked fish, and to skip cut melon that has been in the fridge more than a week or left out more than 2 hours (1 hour above 90°F). If someone in the house is in one of those groups, the chart's days are a ceiling, not a target.

## How to count days, with a dated label example

"3 to 4 days" leaves a question open: does the day you cooked count? The home guidance reviewed here doesn't say. The FDA Food Code rules that licensed kitchens follow do: the day food was prepared, or the day a commercial package was opened, counts as day one. The Champaign-Urbana Public Health District's date-marking page lays this out, along with a second rule: freezing food "stops the date marking clock but does not reset it."

Those restaurant rules use a 7-day limit at 41°F that doesn't apply at home, but the counting method is useful, and it's the conservative reading. Hearth & Habit counts the cook day as day one. Here is one batch of roast chicken worked through with that method and the home figures from the chart.

| Step | Date | Rule used | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cooked, cooled, and in the fridge within 2 hours | Sun, Nov 1, 2026 | Day 1 is the cook day | Day 1 |
| Last day to eat from the fridge (if not frozen) | Wed, Nov 4 | Leftovers 3–4 days | Day 4 |
| Freeze the portion you won't eat | Tue, Nov 3 | Freeze inside the 3–4 day window (Illinois Extension) | Frozen on day 3 |
| Quality target for the freezer | Eat by about Feb 3 to Mar 3, 2027 | Leftovers 3–4 months frozen (FSIS), the conservative end of the 2–6 month range for cooked poultry | Still safe after, at 0°F, but drier |
| Move to the fridge to thaw | Mon, Jan 11, 2027 | Thaw in the refrigerator | Day 1 of the thawed food |
| Last day to eat the thawed chicken | Thu, Jan 14, 2027 | Use within 3–4 days after a fridge thaw (UNL Extension) | Day 4 |

The label on the container would read: "Roast chicken. Cooked Sun 11/1. Frozen Tue 11/3. Best by early Feb." That covers what you need to know when you pull it out.

The table follows home guidance from the University of Nebraska–Lincoln Extension, which gives 3 to 4 days after a fridge thaw. If you'd rather apply the stricter "stops but doesn't reset" logic, the chicken used three of its four days before going into the freezer, which leaves one day after thawing. The way to stop that from mattering is to freeze leftovers on day 1 or 2 rather than on the last day.

A few habits keep the timeline honest:

- **Freeze early, not on the last day.** Illinois Extension says to freeze cooked leftovers within 3 to 4 days, but quality and your margin are both better if you decide on day 1.
- Use shallow containers. UNL says 3 inches deep or less; Illinois says no more than 4 inches. A 3-inch container meets both, and it cools a large batch quickly.
- Don't overload the freezer at once. UNL advises storing only the amount of food that will freeze within 24 hours.
- Reheat leftovers to 165°F on a food thermometer.
- Get rice cold fast. Cooked rice carries spores that survive cooking, so within 1 hour is the safer target for refrigerating it and 2 hours is the outer limit. The reasons are in [why leftover rice makes people sick](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/09/why-leftover-rice-makes-people-sick.html).

More on the 3-to-4-day number is in [how long leftovers really last](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/08/how-long-do-leftovers-really-last.html). Meat thawed in the fridge can be refrozen while still inside its fridge window; meat thawed in cold water or the microwave must be cooked first ([refreezing thawed meat](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/08/is-it-safe-to-refreeze-meat-that-thawed.html)). Big items need their own plan, as in [how long a frozen turkey takes to thaw](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/10/how-long-does-frozen-turkey-take-to.html).

## After a power outage: the keep-or-toss timeline

FDA and FoodSafety.gov give the same three hold times, as long as the doors stay closed: a refrigerator keeps food cold for about 4 hours, a half-full freezer about 24 hours, and a full freezer about 48 hours.

![Bar chart of outage hold times with the doors closed: fridge about 4 hours, half-full freezer about 24 hours, full freezer about 48 hours](/images/fridge-freezer-storage-chart-how-long-food-lasts-diagram.svg)

Those are the outer limits, and they come with a thermometer check. FDA's thermometer page says food that has been above 40°F for only 2 hours or less should be safe, and the FoodSafety.gov keep-or-toss chart sorts food by whether it sat at 40°F or above for more than 2 hours. FoodSafety.gov says to discard refrigerated perishables such as meat, poultry, fish, eggs, and leftovers after 4 hours without power, and FDA draws its discard line at 4 hours or more above 40°F. Put together, the stricter line is the one to use: the fridge gets 4 hours with the door shut, and anything perishable that read above 40°F for more than 2 hours goes.

Here is how the clock plays out for an outage that starts at 6:00 p.m. on a Tuesday, with the doors kept closed:

| Compartment | Hold time | Limit reached |
|---|---|---|
| Refrigerator | About 4 hours | 10:00 p.m. Tuesday |
| Half-full freezer | About 24 hours | 6:00 p.m. Wednesday |
| Full freezer | About 48 hours | 6:00 p.m. Thursday |

And here is what that means if the power returns at three different times:

| Power back | Hours out | Refrigerator | Half-full freezer | Full freezer |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 9:00 p.m. Tuesday | 3 | Within the 4 hours. Keep perishables if the fridge thermometer reads 40°F or below. If it reads higher and you can't show it was there 2 hours or less, use the discard column below. No thermometer reading? Then the 4-hour limit is all you have, and a fridge that stayed shut for 3 hours is inside it. | Well inside 24 hours; check that food still has ice crystals | Well inside 48 hours; check for ice crystals |
| 7:00 a.m. Wednesday | 13 | Past 4 hours: discard perishables. Items on the keep list below can stay. | Inside 24 hours. Refreeze anything with ice crystals or at 40°F or below. | Inside 48 hours. Same check. |
| 12:00 a.m. Thursday (midnight) | 30 | Discard perishables | Past 24 hours. Check every package: refreeze only what has ice crystals or reads 40°F or below; discard the rest using the freezer table. | Inside 48 hours. Refreeze what has ice crystals or reads 40°F or below. |

If an outage looks long, FDA says 50 pounds of dry ice should keep a fully stocked 18-cubic-foot freezer cold for two days. Fill the freezer, keep the doors shut, and have the thermometers in place before the storm rather than after.

### What to keep and what to toss from the fridge

FoodSafety.gov's chart goes item by item for food that sat at 40°F or above for more than 2 hours. Condensed by category:

| Category | Discard | Keep |
|---|---|---|
| Meat, poultry, seafood | Raw or cooked meat, poultry, fish, and seafood; thawing meat; lunch meat, hot dogs, bacon, sausage; meat, tuna, chicken, or egg salads; gravy, stuffing, broth; pizza; opened canned meat and fish | — |
| Cooked dishes | Casseroles, soups, stews; cooked pasta, rice, and potatoes; pasta and potato salads; cooked vegetables; cooked tofu | — |
| Cheese | Soft cheeses (Brie, cottage, cream cheese, mozzarella, ricotta, Monterey Jack and others); shredded cheese; low-fat cheese | Hard cheeses (cheddar, Colby, Swiss, Parmesan, provolone, Romano); processed cheese; grated Parmesan or Romano in a can or jar |
| Dairy and eggs | Milk, cream, sour cream, yogurt, eggnog, soy milk; opened baby formula; shell eggs and egg dishes; custards, puddings, quiche | Butter, margarine |
| Fruit | Cut fresh fruit; sliced or shredded coconut | Uncut fresh fruit; opened fruit juice; opened canned fruit; dried fruit |
| Vegetables | Cut fresh vegetables; pre-cut, pre-washed bagged greens; opened vegetable juice; baked potatoes; commercial garlic in oil | Uncut fresh vegetables; fresh mushrooms, herbs, spices |
| Sauces and spreads | Opened creamy dressings; opened spaghetti sauce; fish sauce, oyster sauce; opened mayonnaise, tartar sauce, horseradish if above 50°F for more than 8 hours | Peanut butter; jelly, relish, mustard, ketchup, olives, pickles; soy, Worcestershire, barbecue, hoisin sauces; opened vinegar-based dressings |
| Bread and dough | Refrigerator biscuit and cookie dough; fresh pasta; cheesecake; cream-filled pastries; pies with egg or milk fillings | Bread, rolls, cakes, muffins, tortillas; waffles, pancakes, bagels; fruit pies |

### What to refreeze and what to toss from the freezer

FoodSafety.gov's rule for the freezer is that food may be refrozen if it still contains ice crystals or reads 40°F or below, though quality may suffer. For packages that sat at 40°F or above for more than 2 hours, most foods are discarded, with some exceptions in both directions:

- Meat, poultry, seafood, soups, stews, casseroles, frozen meals, and frozen pizza: refreeze with ice crystals, discard after more than 2 hours at 40°F or above.
- Ice cream and frozen yogurt: discard either way, even with ice crystals.
- Hard cheeses, fruit juices, packaged fruit, breads and cakes without custard, flour, cornmeal, nuts, waffles, pancakes, and bagels: these can be refrozen even after 2 hours above 40°F (discard fruit if mold, a yeasty smell, or sliminess develops).
- Frozen vegetables: refreeze with ice crystals; discard after 6 hours above 40°F.
- Shredded cheese, soft cheese, cheesecake, and custard- or cheese-filled pastries: refreeze with ice crystals, otherwise discard.

FoodSafety.gov says never to taste food to decide whether it's safe, and FDA says you can't rely on appearance or odor. When the thermometer reading or the timeline is unclear, FoodSafety.gov's advice applies: when in doubt, throw it out.

## When the chart doesn't cover it

Food left out is outside the chart entirely: perishables that sat at room temperature more than 2 hours, or 1 hour above 90°F, get thrown out, not refrigerated or reheated. Buffets have their own timing, covered in [how long party food can sit on a buffet](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/10/how-long-can-party-food-sit-on-buffet.html). A container nobody dated can't be counted, so label everything.
