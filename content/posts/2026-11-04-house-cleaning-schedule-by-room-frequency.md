Wipe the kitchen and bathroom daily, do sinks, floors and sheets weekly, and check filters and alarms monthly. Vent and duct checks, fridge shelves and filter changes come a few times a year; coils only in homes with pets or dust.

Most cleaning schedules stop there, and their intervals rarely say where they came from. Some intervals come from fire-safety agencies, some from appliance manufacturers, some from food-safety research, and some are just habit. This guide labels each one so you know which tasks can slip a week and which can't.

Some of the most important jobs also don't run on a calendar at all. They run per use: every dryer load, every 30 washer loads, every 40 to 80 pots of coffee. Those get their own section, because converting them into dates depends on how your household actually lives.

## The schedule at a glance: room by frequency

Each cell names the source behind the interval. Where the source is "habit," the interval is a reasonable default with no agency or manufacturer number behind it, and you can stretch or shrink it freely.

| Room | Every use or daily | Weekly | Monthly | Every 3 to 12 months |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kitchen | Wipe counters and stovetop after cooking (habit) | Scrub and disinfect the sink, wash the strainers (Illinois Extension); leftover sweep (habit; USDA sets the 3 to 4 day window) | Rinse the dishwasher filter (Whirlpool); sanitize drains and disposal (Illinois Extension); descale the coffeemaker (Illinois Extension) | Wash fridge shelves and drawers (habit, quarterly); condenser coils only in homes with pets or dust, every 3 to 6 months (Whirlpool) |
| Bathroom | Run the fan or open a window while showering (EPA); quick wipe of the sink (habit) | Full clean of toilet, sink and shower (habit; EPA says clean showers more often where moisture builds) | Test GFCI outlets (CPSC) | — |

Read the table by its sources, not just its columns. The safety and manufacturer intervals are the ones to protect when a week goes sideways. The habit intervals are the ones to flex.

## Kitchen: where food safety sets the pace

### The sink, the strainers and the drain

The University of Illinois Extension's kitchen guide says to wash and disinfect the kitchen sink once or twice a week, using a disinfectant cleaner or a solution of 1 tablespoon of regular unscented bleach per gallon of water. It says to wash sink strainers weekly, and to sanitize drains and garbage disposals monthly with 1 tablespoon of bleach in a quart of water.

That bleach step is where a schedule can turn dangerous. Plenty of people also run vinegar through the drain or the coffeemaker, or use a lime-scale or toilet-bowl cleaner nearby. Poison Control warns never to mix chlorine bleach with ammonia or with acids such as vinegar and many toilet-bowl and lime-scale cleaners, because the combination releases toxic chloramine or chlorine gas. Use one product, rinse, then use the other. Follow the bleach label, open a window or run the fan, and wear gloves. Don't put the bleach drain treatment and a vinegar rinse on the same day if you can help it.

A related distinction: soap and water cleans, which removes germs, but it doesn't disinfect. According to the EPA, disinfecting takes an EPA-registered disinfectant left on for its full label contact time. The weekly sink job in the Illinois guide is a disinfecting step, so read the label for the contact time rather than wiping it straight off.

### Sponges and dishcloths

Sponges are the item most schedules get wrong, because some popular fixes did poorly in testing. In a 2007 USDA Agricultural Research Service study, scientists at the agency's Food Technology and Safety Laboratory in Beltsville, Maryland compared five treatments on sponges they had deliberately soaked in a heavy mix of bacteria. Microwaving a sponge for one minute killed more than 99.99 percent of bacteria, and running it through the dishwasher with a drying cycle did nearly as well. The other treatments were a 3-minute soak in 10 percent bleach, a 1-minute soak in lemon juice, a soak in deionized water, and no treatment. Those four groups (including the untreated sponges) showed only 37 to 87 percent fewer bacteria, which the researchers said still left enough to potentially cause disease.

The Illinois Extension guide adds the safety detail that matters most: microwave only a wet sponge, for 2 minutes, because a dry sponge can catch fire. Never microwave a sponge with metal in it, and let it air-dry afterward. If you use the dishwasher instead, the guide says a cycle at 155°F with heated dry is ideal. Illinois also lists a 5-minute bleach soak; in the ARS test on heavily contaminated sponges, a stronger bleach soak left too many bacteria, so heat is the safer choice.

Neither source gives a calendar interval for sponges, so pair the sponge with a job you already do, such as the weekly sink scrub. For dishcloths and potholders, the Illinois guide says to launder them on the hot cycle in the washer and dryer.

### The refrigerator

USDA guidance puts cooked leftovers at 3 to 4 days in a refrigerator held at 40°F or below. USDA sets the window (3 to 4 days at 40°F or below), not the sweep schedule. A weekly sweep is a backstop, so write the date on containers and eat or toss by day 4. If you batch-cook, check twice a week (habit). Smell won't tell you whether something is safe, because the bacteria that cause food poisoning generally don't change how food looks, smells or tastes. Go by the date. For the full set of storage windows, see [our leftovers and fridge storage rules in one place](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/09/leftovers-thawing-and-fridge-storage.html).

For the deeper clean, the Illinois Extension method is:

- Move perishables to a cooler while you work.
- Wash shelves and drawers in hot soapy water.
- Wipe the interior and doors with 2 teaspoons of baking soda per quart of water.
- Leave the door open about 15 minutes to air-dry before you reload.

No source gives a frequency for this deep clean, so the matrix calls it a quarterly habit. If something spills, deal with it the same day.

Condenser coils are the clearest case of a schedule repeating a rule the manufacturer doesn't make. Many lists say "every 6 months" for everyone. Whirlpool's own guidance says that in a normal home, routine condenser cleaning is not typically needed. It recommends inspecting and cleaning every 3 to 6 months only in homes with pets, heavy kitchen traffic or construction dust, and says to unplug the refrigerator or switch off its breaker first. If your fridge smells and you can't find the cause, coils aren't the first suspect. Start with [the fridge-smell troubleshooting order](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/08/why-does-my-fridge-smell-but-i-cant.html).

### Dishwasher, coffeemaker and oven

Whirlpool says to clean the dishwasher filter about once a month, or every two weeks with heavy use. Rinse it under running water with a soft brush, not a wire brush. Other brands set different intervals, so your model's manual wins over any list, including this one. If the dishwasher already smells, the filter is only one of several places to check, and [the dishwasher smell guide](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/08/why-does-my-dishwasher-smell-even-after.html) walks through the rest.

The Illinois Extension guide says to clean a coffeemaker every 40 to 80 brewing cycles, or once a month. Check your machine's manual first. If it has no instructions, Illinois's general method is to run 4 cups of vinegar into the machine, let it stand 30 minutes, then run 2 to 3 cycles of fresh water.

For the oven, the same guide warns against using oven cleaner on a self-cleaning oven.

## Bathroom: moisture sets the pace

Bathroom intervals are mostly habit, with one strong exception: moisture. The EPA's guide to mold and moisture says to run the bathroom fan or open a window when you shower. It also says that in showers and other bathing areas, more ventilation and more frequent cleaning will usually prevent mold from recurring. That's why the matrix puts the fan under "every use." The weekly interval is our habit default; clean sooner if mold keeps coming back.

Three numbers from the EPA help you judge whether the schedule is keeping up:

- Indoor humidity. Keep it below 60 percent, ideally 30 to 50 percent. The EPA says a hygrometer costs about $10 to $50.
- Leaks and spills. Dry wet materials within 24 to 48 hours, and in most cases mold won't grow.
- Mold size. A homeowner can usually clean an area smaller than about 10 square feet, roughly 3 feet by 3 feet. Larger areas, mold in HVAC ducts, or mold after sewage or contaminated water call for a professional.

If a musty smell persists after a weekly clean, the source may not be the bathroom at all. [The room-by-room musty-smell guide](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/09/musty-smell-in-house-how-to-find-source.html) shows where else to look.

Toilet-bowl cleaners are often acidic, which brings back the bleach rule. Don't pour bleach into a bowl that still has toilet-bowl cleaner in it. Flush, rinse, and use one product at a time.

## Bedroom: sheets weekly, towels by condition

In a 2025 Cleveland Clinic article, dermatologist Jennifer Lucas, MD, recommends washing sheets at least once a week, on the hot cycle to help kill bacteria. Use hot if the care label allows. Her reason is what builds up on sheets: shed skin cells, bacteria, dust, pet dander and pollen. She also suggests drying your hair before bed. The article gives no interval for pillows or comforters, so this schedule doesn't invent one.

Towels are different. We found no towel interval from a health agency or manufacturer in our research, so towels aren't in the weekly row. The right interval depends on how fast a towel dries and who uses it. The bath towel guide covers the conditions that matter.

## Laundry room: the fire-safety tasks

This room has the most misplaced interval on typical lists. The lint screen isn't a monthly chore. The Consumer Product Safety Commission says to clean it before or after drying each load.

The vent and exhaust duct are the opposite case. CPSC says to clean them "periodically" and gives no number, so any list that names a firm date is guessing. What CPSC gives instead are the warning signs that the screen or duct may be blocked:

- clothes still damp at the end of a typical cycle;
- drying taking longer than it used to;
- no exhaust air coming out of the outside vent while the dryer runs.

CPSC also says to clean behind the dryer, where lint collects. It recommends replacing plastic or foil accordion duct with rigid or corrugated semi-rigid metal duct, and having a qualified service person clean the inside of the dryer cabinet periodically. The matrix puts a yearly look at the duct and outside hood on the calendar as our assumption, so it doesn't get forgotten. The symptoms above override the calendar: if they show up in March, don't wait for the yearly date.

For washers, LG says to run the Tub Clean cycle on its front-loaders after every 30 uses, or whenever the display shows "tcL". It recommends an oxygen-based washer cleaner and says to avoid chlorine-based or acidic cleaners. If laundry comes out of a clean washer still smelling sour, the musty laundry guide covers the usual causes.

## Whole house: safety checks and the HVAC filter

The monthly safety checks take about ten minutes combined:

- Smoke and CO alarms. NFPA says to test smoke alarms monthly and replace the whole alarm every 10 years. Test carbon monoxide alarms monthly too. If an alarm chirps between tests, here's what the chirp means.
- GFCI outlets. CPSC says to test them monthly with the TEST and RESET buttons.
- HVAC filter. ENERGY STAR says to check the filter every month, especially in heavy-use months in winter and summer. Change it if it looks dirty after a month, and at a minimum every 3 months. It also recommends a yearly tune-up of the heating and cooling equipment. [The furnace filter basics guide](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/07/furnace-filter-basics-how-often-to.html) explains how to read a filter's condition and choose a rating.

The EPA adds a cooling-season item that rarely appears on cleaning lists: keep air-conditioner drip pans clean and drain lines flowing.

Weekly floors and high-touch spots like switches and doorknobs are habit intervals with no published number, so shift them to fit your household.

## Per-use intervals, converted to calendar dates

Two of the cited intervals count uses, not weeks, so the same rule lands on very different dates in different homes. Here's the conversion. The loads and brews per week are example households, not data, so plug in your own.

Washer, LG front-loader, Tub Clean every 30 uses. Weeks between cleanings = 30 ÷ loads per week.

| Loads per week | Arithmetic | Tub Clean every |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | 30 ÷ 3 | 10 weeks |
| 5 | 30 ÷ 5 | 6 weeks |
| 8 | 30 ÷ 8 | about 4 weeks |
| 12 | 30 ÷ 12 | about 2 to 3 weeks |

Coffeemaker, every 40 to 80 cycles or once a month (Illinois Extension):

| Brews per day | Arithmetic | 40 to 80 cycles comes due in |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 40 ÷ 1 to 80 ÷ 1 | 40 to 80 days |
| 2 | 40 ÷ 2 to 80 ÷ 2 | 20 to 40 days |

The tables show two things. A 12-load household needs a tub clean four times as often as a 3-load household (10 weeks ÷ 2.5 weeks), so a flat "monthly" rule over-cleans one and under-cleans the other. For coffee, the once-a-month option arrives before the cycle count for a one-pot household, so the monthly date is the one to put on the calendar. A two-pot household reaches its count somewhere between 20 and 40 days, around the same time.

## How much time this takes: the weekly budget

The schedule is only useful if it fits a week. The table below adds it up. The minutes per task are our planning estimates, not measurements, so replace them with your own if they're off for your home. Monthly jobs are converted to weekly minutes by multiplying by 12 months and dividing by 52 weeks. Yearly totals are divided by 52.

| Tier | Tasks (estimated minutes) | Arithmetic | Minutes per week |
|---|---|---|---|
| Daily | Kitchen reset 7 + bathroom wipe 3 = 10 a day | 10 × 7 | 70 |
| Weekly | Sink, strainers and sponge 10 + leftover sweep 5 + bathroom 20 + sheets 10 + floors 30 + switches and knobs 10 | sum | 85 |
| Monthly | Dishwasher filter 5 + drains 5 + start washer clean 2 + coffeemaker 5 + smoke and CO alarms 5 + GFCIs 3 + HVAC filter look 2 = 27 a month | 27 × 12 ÷ 52 | 6.2 |
| Quarterly to yearly | HVAC filter 5 × 4 + fridge shelves 30 × 4 + dryer duct and hood 30 × 1 + coils 15 × 2 = 200 a year | 200 ÷ 52 | 3.8 |
| Total | | 70 + 85 + 6.2 + 3.8 | about 165 (2.75 hours) |

A few notes on the inputs:

- The coffeemaker's 30-minute vinegar soak and its water cycles run unattended, so only hands-on minutes are counted.
- The washer's tub clean is counted as monthly, which matches about 7 loads a week (30 ÷ 7 is about 4 weeks).
- The coil line applies only to homes with pets or heavy dust, per Whirlpool. Drop it and the yearly tier falls to 170 ÷ 52, about 3.3 minutes a week.
- The sponge is folded into the sink job's 10 minutes; the microwave or dishwasher does the work.
- Sheets count only the 10 hands-on minutes to strip, load and remake the bed. The rest of the household laundry isn't in the budget, and neither is the yearly HVAC tune-up, which a technician does.

**The monthly and yearly jobs people dread add up to about 10 minutes a week. The daily and weekly basics take the other 155.** All of those jobs together take about a third of the time of weekly floor cleaning (10 versus 30 minutes a week), so putting the filter and alarm checks on a calendar costs almost nothing.

How does 165 minutes compare with real households? The Bureau of Labor Statistics' American Time Use Survey for 2025 puts interior cleaning at 0.33 hours a day, averaged across everyone age 15 and older:

- 0.33 × 60 = 19.8 minutes a day.
- 19.8 × 7 = 138.6 minutes a week, about 2.3 hours.

Not a like-for-like comparison: ATUS counts one category, and this plan's minutes are our estimates. Use it only as a sense of scale. On those terms, this plan is about 26 minutes a week more than that average for one person, or about 83 minutes each (165 ÷ 2) if two adults split it. Laundry is tracked separately in the survey at 0.17 hours a day, about 71 minutes a week.

If 85 weekly minutes still feels like too much in one go, [the 15-minute weekly reset](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/07/the-15-minute-weekly-home-reset-simple.html) breaks the weekly tier into short sessions. And before you buy anything for this schedule, [the cleaning supplies you actually need](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/07/cleaning-supplies-you-actually-need-and.html) keeps the shelf short.

## Printable checklist

Copy this into a notes app or print it. The source tags match the matrix above.

Every load or every use

- ☐ Dryer lint screen (CPSC)
- ☐ Bathroom fan on while showering (EPA)

Daily

- ☐ Wipe kitchen counters and stovetop (habit)
- ☐ Quick wipe of bathroom sink (habit)

Weekly

- ☐ Scrub and disinfect kitchen sink; wash strainers (Illinois Extension)
- ☐ Microwave the wet sponge for 2 minutes, or run it through the dishwasher (timing is habit; method from USDA ARS and Illinois Extension)
- ☐ Leftover sweep: toss anything past day 4; twice a week if you batch-cook (habit; 3 to 4 day window from USDA)
- ☐ Clean toilet, sink and shower (habit, EPA)
- ☐ Change and wash sheets, hot if the care label allows (Cleveland Clinic)
- ☐ Floors, light switches, doorknobs (habit)

Monthly

- ☐ Test smoke and CO alarms (NFPA)
- ☐ Test GFCI outlets (CPSC)
- ☐ Look at the HVAC filter; change it if dirty (ENERGY STAR)
- ☐ Rinse the dishwasher filter, or every 2 weeks with heavy use (Whirlpool)
- ☐ Sanitize drains and disposal (Illinois Extension); no vinegar or acid cleaners the same day
- ☐ Descale the coffeemaker (Illinois Extension)
- ☐ Washer tub clean if you're near 30 loads (LG)

Every 3 months to yearly

- ☐ Change the HVAC filter, at least every 3 months (ENERGY STAR)
- ☐ Wash fridge shelves and drawers (habit)
- ☐ Fridge coils, only with pets or dust, every 3 to 6 months (Whirlpool)
- ☐ Clean behind the dryer; check the duct, and that air comes out of the outside vent while it runs (CPSC)
- ☐ HVAC tune-up (ENERGY STAR)

Every 10 years

- ☐ Replace smoke alarms (NFPA)

If the house slides back into clutter faster than this schedule can keep up, the problem is usually storage rather than cleaning. [Matching the clutter problem to its fix](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/09/clutter-keeps-coming-back-match-problem.html) is the place to start.
