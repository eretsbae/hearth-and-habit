Count back from your area's median first 32°F freeze, not the hard-freeze date: book furnace and chimney service six weeks out, seal and insulate four weeks out, and drain outdoor water lines the last weekend before.

Most winter checklists list tasks in no particular order, but the order matters. Some jobs need a technician's calendar or a part you have to buy; others can't happen until you've watered the lawn for the last time. This guide sorts every task by lead time, shows what skipping each can cost using insurer and fire-service data, and ends with a printable checklist.

If your first freeze has already come and gone, start with the late-start triage near the end.

## Plan to the 32°F date, not the hard-freeze date

The National Weather Service draws a line between the two. Its St. Louis office defines a freeze as the temperature falling to 32°F or below and a hard freeze as 28°F or below. Those definitions are written around plants. Your water lines care about the first one: water freezes at 32°F, as Colorado State University Extension notes in its sprinkler guidance.

The gap between the two dates is wider than most people expect. The Weather Service's 1991–2020 climatology for St. Louis puts the median first freeze on Nov. 1 and the median first hard freeze on Nov. 15. Plan your outdoor-water jobs around the hard-freeze date and you'd leave hoses connected and sprinkler lines full through the roughly two weeks between the two median dates.

Two more things in those tables change how you should plan:

- A median is a coin flip. In about half of years, the first freeze comes before the median date. St. Louis's earliest first freeze on record was Sept. 28, more than a month ahead of its Nov. 1 median.
- Nearby places differ a lot. Columbia, Missouri, covered by the same Weather Service office, has a median first freeze of Oct. 22, ten days earlier than St. Louis. Quincy, Illinois, sits at Oct. 19. Use the station closest to you, not the nearest big city.

To find your own date, check your local Weather Service office's frost and freeze page and write down the median first 32°F date. Everything below counts back from it.

## What skipping each job can cost

The two expensive failures in a house over winter are water and heat: pipes that split, and heating equipment that starts a fire or leaks carbon monoxide.

The Insurance Information Institute (Triple-I) publishes how often homeowners file each type of claim and what the average claim costs. Multiply the two and you get a rough expected cost per insured home per year, which lets you compare risks that are frequent but modest with ones that are rare but severe.

| Cause of loss (Triple-I, 2019–2023) | Claims per 100 insured homes per year | Average claim | Expected cost per home per year |
|---|---|---|---|
| Water damage and freezing | 1.50 | $15,400 | 1.50 ÷ 100 × $15,400 = $231 |
| Fire and lightning | 0.23 | $88,170 | 0.23 ÷ 100 × $88,170 ≈ $203 |
| Wind and hail | 2.80 | $14,747 | 2.80 ÷ 100 × $14,747 ≈ $413 |

*Inputs are Triple-I's weighted averages for insured homes; the last column is our arithmetic. Water damage and freezing are reported together, so the first row includes leaks that have nothing to do with cold.*

**Water claims are more than six times as common as fire claims, so their expected cost per home is higher even though a fire claim averages more than five times as much.** And at 1.50 claims per 100 homes, roughly 1 in 67 insured homes (100 ÷ 1.50) files a water-damage or freezing claim each year.

State Farm publishes its own freeze-only figures. They measure something different (one insurer's payouts, not Triple-I's industry claim averages), so the two aren't directly comparable. State Farm says that from 2024 through June 2025 it paid more than 20,000 freeze-related claims totaling over $628 million, with an average payment over $30,000. Illinois, Oregon, Washington, Texas and Tennessee had the highest totals, a reminder that the South and the mild Pacific Northwest are not exempt.

On the heating side, the U.S. Fire Administration (USFA) counts an average of 34,200 heating fires a year in residential buildings for 2017–2019, causing 165 deaths a year. Seventy-seven percent of those fires were confined to a chimney, flue or fuel burner, the parts that the annual professional cleaning and inspection the USFA recommends covers. Carbon monoxide adds its own toll: the CDC says more than 400 people die each year from unintentional CO poisoning not linked to fires, and more than 100,000 visit an emergency department.

## The lead-time table

|---|---|---|---|
| 6 weeks out | Book furnace, boiler and water heater service | Needs a technician appointment | CDC: fuel-burning appliances should be serviced by a qualified technician every year; more than 400 non-fire CO deaths a year |
| 6 weeks out | Book chimney inspection and cleaning (if you burn wood or have a fuel-burning flue) | Needs a sweep's appointment | USFA: 77% of residential heating fires are confined to chimneys, flues or fuel burners |
| 6 weeks out | Test smoke and CO alarms; replace expired ones | May need to buy alarms | NFPA: replace smoke alarms every 10 years; CDC: CO detectors near every sleeping area |

## Weekend 1, six weeks out: jobs that need someone else's calendar

### Furnace, boiler and water heater service

The CDC's carbon monoxide guidance is specific: have your heating system, water heater and any other gas, oil or coal appliances serviced by a qualified technician every year. The USFA says the same for heating equipment and chimneys: cleaned and inspected each year by a professional. Changing the filter is your job, not a substitute for the service visit. If you're unsure which filter or how often, start with [furnace filter basics and what MERV means](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/07/furnace-filter-basics-how-often-to.html).

Ask for the visit before your median first-freeze date, so any part that needs ordering arrives before you depend on the heat. If the system smells when you first fire it up, [this step-by-step guide](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/10/why-does-my-furnace-smell-like-burning.html) covers which smells need a call.

### Chimney and flue

If you burn wood or have any fuel-burning appliance venting through a chimney, the CDC says to have the chimney checked or cleaned every year. The USFA's numbers explain why it's in the first weekend: in its 2017–2019 data, 77% of residential heating fires were confined to a chimney, flue or fuel burner, and heating fires peaked in January. Our editorial view: get the inspection done before that peak.

### Smoke and CO alarms

Test every smoke alarm and CO alarm now (the NFPA's standing advice is to test monthly and replace a smoke alarm every 10 years). The CDC adds two placement and timing points that many checklists skip: CO detectors should be battery-operated or have battery backup, they belong near every sleeping area, and their batteries get checked when the clocks change each spring and fall.

Look at the date on the back of each smoke alarm before you test it. If it's due for replacement, you need to buy one, which is why this sits in weekend 1.

### The main water shutoff

The Insurance Institute for Business & Home Safety (IBHS) tells homeowners to know where the home's water shut-off valve is and how to operate it. Utah State University Extension goes further: teach adults and older children where it is and how to close it. A burst pipe at 2 a.m. is the wrong time to search the basement with a flashlight.

Find it now and turn it to confirm it moves. If it's seized, call a plumber while there's still time.

## Weekend 2, four weeks out: seal, insulate, replace

### Old washer and dishwasher hoses

IBHS's winter guide includes a task almost no other checklist mentions: change water supply hoses on washing machines and dishwashers that are more than 10 years old. It has nothing to do with outdoor temperature, but it's a pressurized water line that fails indoors, which is the kind of water claim in Triple-I's table above.

### Attic air sealing, the real ice-dam fix

IBHS explains that ice dams form when attic heat warms the upper roof deck, so the fix lives in the attic, not the gutters. State Farm's prevention list shows where to look:

- Seal attic hatches and whole-house fans
- Add insulation to the attic floor
- Replace recessed lights with sealed IC-rated fixtures
- Seal and insulate HVAC ducts with mastic and foil-faced fiberglass
- Caulk around cables, vents and other spots where air escapes
- Ventilate eaves and ridges so cold air circulates under the roof

If a past winter left a stain on an upper-floor ceiling near an outside wall, treat it as a clue worth following up before the next freeze.

### Exterior gaps, windows and doors

IBHS says to caulk and seal cracks and gaps on the home's exterior, including around windows and doors. [Caulking 101](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/07/caulking-101-where-to-caulk-what-to-buy.html) covers which caulk goes where. If one room never warms up, find out why before you seal it in; [this guide to one cold room](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/09/why-is-one-room-in-my-house-always.html) lists the usual causes, including drafts and duct problems.

### Exposed pipes, and what insulation can't do

IBHS says to insulate pipes in attics, crawl spaces and along exterior walls. State Farm names the same three places as the most at risk, especially below 20°F outside.

USU Extension says insulation must completely cover the pipes, fittings and fixtures exposed to cold, and must go on only after any leaks are fixed, or the wet insulation stops working. It also sets the limit plainly: insulation won't protect pipes from prolonged freezing temperatures unless there is also some source of heat, periodic water flow, or both. Foam sleeves buy time on a cold night. They are not a reason to skip the freeze-night steps below. Hot-water lines in the same cold space need sleeves too, because, as USU points out, they freeze just like cold lines.

## Weekend 3, two weeks out: gutters, downspouts and generator plan

### Gutters and downspouts

Leaves have to be mostly down before a gutter cleaning lasts, which is why this job sits late. IBHS's goal is simple: clear debris from gutters and downspouts so water flows away from the home. Then check where it lands. The Department of Energy's building-science guidance is to discharge downspouts at least 5 feet from the foundation, onto ground that slopes away from the house; about a 6-inch drop over the first 10 feet is the usual target.

If your gutters have guards, they still need a look. For the rest of the yard, [the fall yard cleanup checklist](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/07/fall-yard-cleanup-checklist-what.html) sorts which jobs matter before winter and which can wait until spring.

### Generator placement and outage nights

A freeze becomes dangerous when the power goes out with it. If you own a generator, decide where it will run before you need it, in daylight. The CDC says to operate it outdoors more than 20 feet from windows, doors and vents, and Ready.gov's distance rule adds attached garages, so never run one inside an attached garage. The USFA adds one more rule for outage nights: never use an oven to heat your home.

## Weekend 4, the last weekend before your median freeze: outdoor water

This is the only weekend that has to wait. Hoses, outdoor faucets and sprinkler lines all hold water, and Colorado State Extension notes that water expands by one-eleventh when it freezes: 11 cubic feet of water becomes about 12 cubic feet of ice. Something has to give, and it's usually the pipe or fitting.

### Hoses and outdoor faucets

State Farm's list starts with the basics: disconnect garden hoses and drain outdoor pipes. IBHS adds the detail that decides how much work you have: if you don't have frost-proof outdoor faucets, shut off and insulate those valves, and homes built before 2010 typically don't have them. For how to tell which faucet you have and where the inside valve usually hides, see [should you shut off the outdoor faucet from inside](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/10/should-you-shut-off-outdoor-faucet-from.html).

### Sprinkler blowout

Generic lists say "blow out the sprinklers." Colorado State Extension's procedure has the details that keep you from breaking something:

1. Turn off the supply at the stop-and-waste valve, without forcing it.
2. Don't trust manual or automatic drain valves to empty the lines. Blow them out with compressed air.
3. Keep compressor pressure in the 40–80 psi range, with 80 psi the maximum for rigid PVC pipe and 50 psi for flexible black polyethylene pipe.
4. Blow out each zone with two short cycles rather than one long one.
5. Never blow compressed air through a pressure vacuum breaker (PVB) or reduced-pressure (RP) backflow device. The rubber seals inside can melt from the heat of the air.
6. Leave the backflow device's ball valves and test cocks half-open for winter. Fully open or fully closed valves trap water, which expands and causes damage.
7. Wear eye protection.

If you don't know your pipe type or backflow device, hire the blowout; Colorado State notes that fall preparation costs much less than spring repairs. While you're out there, the last lawn mow is a fall job too; [the fall-to-spring yard calendar](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/11/fall-to-spring-yard-calendar-month-by.html) shows when it falls for your grass type.

### Pools

State Farm's guidance for pool owners is to winterize by draining the equipment and turning off freeze guards.

## Freeze night: the last three steps

When a 32°F low shows up in the forecast, three things from State Farm's list take minutes:

- Heat: keep the home heated to at least 55°F. IBHS gives the same floor, even when no one is home. If you use a programmable schedule, make sure no setback period dips below that.
- Cabinets: open the doors under sinks on exterior walls so room heat reaches the pipes.
- Faucets: let faucets fed by exposed pipes drip on cold nights to keep water moving.

If you add a space heater to warm a cold spot, the USFA says to keep anything that can burn at least 3 feet away from it and to turn it off when you leave the room or go to bed. Its 2017–2019 numbers show why: portable heaters caused only 3% of home heating fires but 41% of the fatal ones. For what running one costs, see [how much it costs to run a space heater for 8 hours](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/10/how-much-does-it-cost-to-run-space.html). For the full forecast-by-pipe-location breakdown and how to thaw a pipe safely, see [at what temperature pipes actually freeze](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/10/at-what-temperature-do-pipes-freeze.html).

### Leaving for the holidays

IBHS says that if you'll be away longer than a week while subfreezing temperatures are ongoing or forecast, consider closing the main valve slowly and letting a couple of faucets on the lowest floor drain. State Farm adds the tradeoff: shutting off the water also disables home fire sprinklers. Renters should check with the landlord before shutting off or draining anything, since a building's main valve may serve more than one unit.

## Already past your first freeze? Do it in this order

It's early November, so in plenty of places the median first freeze has already passed. You haven't missed the window; you've lost the slack. Reorder the list by what fails first:

1. Outdoor water, today. Disconnect hoses, close the inside valves to standard outdoor faucets and drain them, and drain or blow out irrigation lines.
2. Main shutoff. Know where it is before the first pipe problem, not during it.
3. Heating safety. Test smoke and CO alarms now and book the furnace and chimney visit for the first available slot. If you burn wood, hold off until the chimney is inspected.
4. Exposed pipes. Sleeves on pipes in the crawl space, attic or garage, then the freeze-night routine whenever a 32°F low is forecast.
5. Everything else. Air sealing, washer hoses and gutters can follow over the next few weekends.

## The printable one-page checklist

Write your median first 32°F date at the top, then subtract to get each weekend (the offsets are our editorial schedule). Here is the arithmetic for two stations in the Weather Service's St. Louis tables:

| Weekend | Count back | St. Louis (median first freeze Nov. 1) | Columbia, Mo. (median first freeze Oct. 22) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Median − 42 days | Sept. 20 | Sept. 10 |
| 2 | Median − 28 days | Oct. 4 | Sept. 24 |
| 3 | Median − 14 days | Oct. 18 | Oct. 8 |
| 4 | Median − 7 days | Oct. 25 | Oct. 15 |

Use the nearest Saturday or Sunday to each date. One more check makes the plan honest: St. Louis's earliest recorded first freeze (Sept. 28) and Columbia's (Sept. 18) both land before weekend 2. From weekend 1 on, watch the forecast. If a 32°F low shows up early, do the weekend-4 outdoor-water jobs that day and pick up the rest of the schedule afterward.

My median first 32°F freeze date: ____________

### Weekend 1 (6 weeks out): date ________
- [ ] Book furnace, boiler and water heater service
- [ ] Book chimney inspection and cleaning, if you use one
- [ ] Test every smoke and CO alarm; check smoke alarm dates
- [ ] Add CO alarms near every sleeping area if missing
- [ ] Find the main water shutoff and turn it once

### Weekend 2 (4 weeks out): date ________
- [ ] Replace washer and dishwasher supply hoses over 10 years old
- [ ] Seal attic hatch, fan and light openings; add attic-floor insulation if thin
- [ ] Caulk exterior gaps around windows and doors
- [ ] Sleeve exposed pipes in attic, crawl space and exterior walls, fittings included

### Weekend 3 (2 weeks out): date ________
- [ ] Clear gutters and downspouts
- [ ] Extend downspouts to discharge at least 5 feet from the foundation
- [ ] Pick the generator spot: outdoors, more than 20 feet from windows, doors and vents, never in an attached garage

### Weekend 4 (last weekend before the median): date ________
- [ ] Disconnect and drain garden hoses
- [ ] Close inside valves to standard outdoor faucets and open the outside faucets to drain
- [ ] Blow out irrigation lines (or book the blowout); never blow air through the backflow device
- [ ] Drain pool equipment and turn off freeze guards

### Every freeze night
- [ ] Heat at 55°F or more, including any setback or away setting
- [ ] Open sink cabinets on exterior walls
- [ ] Drip faucets fed by exposed pipes
- [ ] Space heaters 3 feet from anything that can burn, off when you leave the room
