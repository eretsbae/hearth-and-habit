It comes back because cleaning removes the growth but leaves the conditions behind: a film of soap, water that sits, and air that stays damp. Pink film is usually a bacterium feeding on soap residue, and black specks are usually mold, so each needs a slightly different fix.

Gwinnett County's water department says the pink organism usually can't be wiped out for good once it's established. The US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) says a damp bathroom is hard to keep completely free of mold. The realistic goal is control: take away the food and the moisture so regrowth stays slow and small. Three common beliefs get in the way of that.

## Belief: the pink stuff is a kind of mold

It's a bacterium. North Dakota State University Extension's water quality associate identifies the red film in tubs and toilets as *Serratia marcescens*. McGill University's Office for Science and Society explains that the color is a pigment the bacteria make as they multiply.

What it eats explains why it comes back. NDSU says the bacteria grow where materials containing phosphorus or fatty substances collect, and soap residue in the tub, shower and sink is one of the examples it names. McGill adds the second half: the bacteria can't survive in chlorinated tap water, but when water sits on grout or a shower curtain, the chlorine evaporates and the bacteria take root, especially where soapy deposits give them food.

So a shower that gets a fresh coat of soap film every day restocks the pantry every day. A few other details from the same sources fill in the rest:

- **It arrives from the air.** Gwinnett County's water department calls these airborne bacteria found in dust and soil. NDSU says the film shows up more when windows are open in summer and during or after construction or remodeling.
- **Carbon filters can make it worse.** NDSU and Gwinnett both say an activated carbon filter strips chlorine from the water, which may support growth. If the film appeared after you added a carbon filter to your water line, that's worth considering.
- **Scratches give it a foothold.** Gwinnett warns that abrasives that scratch fixtures make them even more susceptible to the bacteria. Scouring harder can leave the surface easier to recolonize.

Gwinnett notes that *Serratia* is not known to cause waterborne disease. It has caused urinary tract infections, wound infections and pneumonia in a small percentage of people in hospital settings, and McGill says those infections mostly affect people with weakened immune systems.

## Belief: once you bleach it, it's gone

For black mold spots, EPA's advice differs from what many cleaning guides lead with. Its brief guide to mold says chlorine bleach and other biocides are not recommended as a routine practice in mold cleanup. Its reasoning is the useful part: dead mold may still cause allergic reactions, so killing it isn't enough. It has to be removed. EPA's cleanup page says to scrub mold off hard surfaces with detergent and water, then dry them completely.

EPA's point is that killing isn't the same as removing. A spray that whitens a stain without scrubbing can leave material behind, which is one reason grout can look clean on Sunday and spotted again by the next weekend.

For the pink film, NDSU and Gwinnett do recommend chlorine, but as a step after scrubbing. Gwinnett's routine is to clean the surface, apply a strong chlorine bleach solution, leave it 10 to 20 minutes, then rinse thoroughly. NDSU gives the same 10-to-20-minute contact time. Even then, Gwinnett says cleaning with chlorine won't necessarily eliminate the problem. It only controls it, and you repeat the process whenever a pink film starts to reappear.

If you use bleach, follow the poison-control rule: never mix chlorine bleach with ammonia or with acids such as vinegar and many lime-scale cleaners, because the combination releases toxic gas. Use one product, rinse, then use the other. EPA also says to ventilate the room whenever you use a disinfectant. Our guide to [the cleaning supplies you actually need](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/07/cleaning-supplies-you-actually-need-and.html) covers which products not to combine.

### Pink vs. black at a glance

| | Pink film | Black specks |
|---|---|---|
| What it usually is | *Serratia marcescens*, a bacterium (NDSU, McGill) | Mold (EPA) |
| What feeds it | Soap residue and other fatty or phosphorus-containing deposits, in water that has lost its chlorine | Moisture on surfaces. EPA says no mold grows without water or moisture. |
| Cleaning the sources describe | Scrub, then chlorine bleach solution for 10–20 minutes, then rinse (Gwinnett, NDSU) | Scrub with detergent and water, then dry completely. Bleach isn't routine. (EPA) |
| What keeps it down | Wipe tubs and sinks dry after use; repeat cleaning when film reappears | More ventilation and more frequent cleaning (EPA) |
| When it's more than a cleaning job | Film keeps showing up after a carbon filter was added | Mold covering more than about 10 square feet, or on porous material that won't come clean |

## Belief: flipping the fan on during the shower is enough

EPA's own answer to recurring shower mold is simple: increasing ventilation and cleaning more often will usually keep mold from coming back, or at least keep it to a minimum. The details that decide whether your ventilation counts are run time, fan size, and where the air goes.

![Diagram: soap film feeds pink bacteria and standing water lets them take root, damp grout grows mold, and drying plus an outdoor-vented fan break the cycle](/images/shower-grout-pink-black-comes-back-inline.svg)

**Run time.** The two main sources disagree, and that's useful to know. The Home Ventilating Institute (HVI) recommends leaving the fan on for 20 minutes after using the bathroom. The Building America guide from the Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL) recommends running it for one hour after a shower for effective humidity control. PNNL suggests a timer switch so the fan shuts itself off. Since the sources differ, a reasonable approach is to start at 20 minutes and move toward an hour if spots keep returning.

**Where the air goes.** PNNL says the fan must be ducted to the outdoors, not into the attic, crawlspace, garage or wall cavities. It also says an openable window shouldn't be relied on for consistent bathroom ventilation. A fan that dumps moist air into the attic only moves the problem.

**Size.** This is where many bathrooms fall short, and you can check yours with a tape measure.

### Worked example: is your fan big enough?

Here are the inputs. HVI recommends 1 cfm (cubic foot per minute) per square foot for bathrooms up to 100 square feet. Above 100 square feet, it adds up 50 cfm each for a toilet, a shower and a tub (100 for a jetted tub). PNNL lists a minimum of 50 cfm for a fan you switch on and off. It adds that ENERGY STAR recommends buying a fan rated at least 70 cfm to deliver that 50.

| Bathroom | Floor area | HVI target | Minimum to meet | What to look for on the box |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5 × 8 ft, tub/shower | 5 × 8 = 40 sq ft | 40 × 1 = 40 cfm | 50 cfm floor is higher, so 50 | Rated 70 cfm or more (ENERGY STAR) |
| 9 × 10 ft, shower | 9 × 10 = 90 sq ft | 90 × 1 = 90 cfm | 90 cfm | At least 90 cfm rated. The sources give no added margin at this size, so size up if the duct run is long. |
| 10 × 12 ft, toilet + shower + separate tub | 10 × 12 = 120 sq ft (over 100) | 50 + 50 + 50 = 150 cfm | 150 cfm | At least 150 cfm rated. No added margin is given at this size either. |

The small-bathroom row is the surprise. By square footage a 40-square-foot bath "needs" only 40 cfm. But the 50 cfm floor applies, and ENERGY STAR's advice to buy a 70 cfm fan to get 50 implies a fan can deliver less than its label says. ENERGY STAR's figure is given only for that 50 cfm case, so the larger rows show the target with no margin added.

To use your own numbers, multiply length by width. If the result is 100 square feet or less, that's your cfm target, but never below 50 for a fan you switch on and off. If it's over 100, add 50 for each toilet, shower and tub instead. HVI also says the bathroom door needs a gap at the bottom so replacement air can get in. A fan pulling against a tight, closed door moves less.

## What actually keeps grout clean longer

Put the three corrections together and the routine looks like this:

1. **Remove, don't just whiten.** Scrub the film off with a cleaner and a brush that won't scratch the surface, and rinse. For pink film, follow with the 10-to-20-minute chlorine soak and rinse the utilities describe. For black specks, EPA says detergent and water, then dry.
2. **Dry the surfaces after each shower.** NDSU and Gwinnett both say to keep tubs and sinks wiped down and dry. That takes away the standing water the bacteria need. Wipe the corners and the low grout lines where water pools.
3. **Run the fan long enough.** That means at least HVI's 20 minutes, or PNNL's hour if regrowth keeps winning, on a fan that vents outdoors and is sized for the room.
4. **Watch the humidity.** EPA says to keep indoor humidity below 60%, ideally 30–50%. A cheap hygrometer in the bathroom tells you whether the fan is clearing the room.
5. **Know when it's not the surface.** Under about 10 square feet of mold is usually a DIY job, according to EPA. Above that, EPA's guidance points to a professional, and our guide to [tracking down a musty smell room by room](https://www.hearth-habit.com/2026/09/musty-smell-in-house-how-to-find-source.html) can help find hidden moisture.

One more caution from EPA: don't caulk over moldy surfaces. Clean and dry them first. EPA also says mold can fill the crevices of porous materials, so it may be hard or impossible to remove completely. EPA's examples are ceiling tiles and carpet. Applying the same logic to caulk is our judgment, not EPA's: a bead that stays spotted after a thorough cleaning is a candidate for replacement rather than another scrub.
