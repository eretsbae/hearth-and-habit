Your windows fog because the inside of the glass has dropped below the dew point of your room air, which happens most after the coldest part of the night. The colder it gets outside, the less humidity your windows can carry before they drip.

That second sentence is the part most advice skips. A single humidity target for the whole winter doesn't work, because the limit moves with the forecast. The same 40 percent that left the glass dry in November can fog it on the first subzero morning in January.

## Why mornings, and why the bottom of the glass

The National Research Council of Canada (NRC) puts it simply: condensation forms on the inside of a window whenever the glass surface falls below the dew point of the room. The dew point is the temperature at which the air can't hold its moisture anymore. In a companion digest on humidity, NRC gives an example: room air at 75°F and 40 percent relative humidity (RH) becomes saturated when it cools to 49°F against a window. Any glass colder than that collects water.

Three things push the glass below that line by morning:

- **The coldest part of the night has just passed.** A GreenBuildingAdvisor case study describes the pattern: condensation forms in the morning, after the coldest part of the night, when indoor humidity is too high for the outdoor temperature.
- **The bottom of a double window is colder than the top.** NRC explains that air circulating inside a double window leaves the bottom of the air space several degrees colder, which is why condensation on double windows usually shows up at the bottom first. NRC adds that the interior sill (the "stool") interferes with air flow at the bottom of the window.
- **Frames and edges run cold.** NRC notes that aluminum conducts heat about 800 to 1,400 times better than wood. An aluminum frame without a thermal break condenses at roughly the humidity limit of single glass, even if the glass itself is double.

When the glass stays below freezing for long stretches, NRC says the water collects as frost instead.

## How much humidity your windows can carry at tonight's low

This table is NRC's calculation of the highest indoor RH a window can carry without condensation at the center of the glass. It assumes 70°F indoors and no sun. "Windy" means a 15 mph wind outside, and "calm" means no wind.

| Tonight's outdoor low | Single pane (windy / calm) | Double pane (windy / calm) | Triple pane (windy / calm) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 40°F | 44% / 59% | 68% / 73% | 78% / 81% |
| 20°F | 24% / 41% | 53% / 61% | 68% / 71% |
| 0°F | 12% / 27% | 41% / 49% | 57% / 61% |
| -20°F | 6% / 17% | 32% / 39% | 49% / 53% |
| -40°F | 2% / 10% | 23% / 31% | 41% / 46% |

To use it, read your humidity on a hygrometer (EPA says these meters cost about $10 to $50). Then find tonight's low and your window type. If your reading is above the number in that cell, expect fog by morning.

### Worked example: a double-pane house at 40 percent

- At a 20°F low with wind, the double-pane limit is 53%. A 40% reading is 13 points under it, so the glass should stay clear.
- At 0°F with wind, the limit is 41%. That leaves 1 point of margin at the center of the glass, and the bottom edge runs colder than the center, so fog there is likely.
- At -20°F with wind, the limit is 32%. The reading is now 8 points over, so expect condensation on the glass.

Your windows probably fog sooner than the table says. The University of Minnesota's publication on winter humidity warns that these figures describe the center of the glass and ignore the spacers and frames. Its own table puts sealed double glazing at 32 to 37 percent at 0°F, well under NRC's 41 percent. **Treat the table as a ceiling, not a target: real windows fog a few points below it.**

## Where the moisture comes from

Montana State University Extension estimates that an average family of four can add over six gallons of water to the air each day. Its itemized list, in pounds per day:

| Source | Pounds per day |
|---|---|
| Clothes dryer vented into the house | 26.4 |
| Breathing and perspiring | 16.8 |
| Washing clothes | 4.3 |
| Mopping floors | 2.4 |
| Cooking on an electric range, no exhaust fan | 2.0 |
| Shower or bath | 2.0 |
| Dishwashing by hand | 1.0 |
| House plants | 1.0 |
| **Total** | **55.9 (6.7 gallons)** |

The dryer is the one line you can remove outright. Montana's total implies about 8.34 pounds per gallon (55.9 ÷ 6.7). Venting the dryer outside takes the total from 55.9 to 29.5 pounds a day (55.9 − 26.4), or about 3.5 gallons (29.5 ÷ 8.34). Breathing and perspiring, at 16.8 pounds, is the next-largest line. EPA adds that unvented combustion appliances, such as kerosene heaters, release water vapor into the room.

## Comparing the fixes

Each fix works on one of two levers: less moisture in the air, or warmer glass.

| Fix | What it does | Where it falls short |
|---|---|---|
| Turn the humidifier down or off | NDSU Extension says to shut humidifiers off to lower humidity | Below 30% RH, NDSU says, skin dries and nasal passages get irritated |
| Run bath and kitchen exhaust fans; vent the dryer outdoors | Removes moisture at the source. Montana says fans must exhaust outside, not into the attic or crawl space | Breathing and plants still add moisture |
| Portable dehumidifier | Pulls water from the air | NDSU says they generally reach only about 50% RH, too high to stop winter window condensation |
| Plastic film or a storm window | Warms the inner glass. Montana says a storm needs at least a ½-inch gap and sealed edges | The University of Minnesota notes that if the sash isn't tightly weatherstripped, or the storm is very tight, condensation can form on the storm window |
| Keep air moving across the glass | Lets warm room air reach the glass. Montana says not to cover supply or return registers, and to use a ceiling fan | Doesn't help if humidity is far over the limit; whether to close coverings at night is disputed (below) |
| New double or triple glazing | Raises the humidity ceiling, as the table shows | NRC says frames need thermal breaks or they condense at roughly single-glass limits |

### Curtains closed or open at night?

The sources disagree, and the disagreement is useful. Montana State says to close drapes at night so moist air can't reach the cold glass. The University of Minnesota says curtains, blinds, and interior screens that restrict air currents can cause condensation at lower humidity. NRC's answer is that coverings can increase or decrease condensation, depending on where they sit relative to the window and the heating outlet.

In practice, your own windows are the test. If the fog is consistently worst behind closed blinds or curtains, the University of Minnesota's explanation fits your house: try leaving them open, and keep register air moving across the glass. NRC notes that a heat source beneath a window partly offsets the cold bottom edge.

### Should you raise the humidity back up?

Not past the fog line. NRC is blunt about it: any moisture you add above the point where visible condensation starts just moves to the cold surfaces and collects there. A smart humidistat with an outdoor sensor, described in the GreenBuildingAdvisor case study, lowers the setpoint automatically on cold nights. You can do the same by hand from the table.

## When the fog is a warning, not just a nuisance

Water that sits on sills, frames, and paint does damage. Montana State says excessive moisture that condenses on cold surfaces can make paint peel, wood rot, and mildew grow. EPA's advice is to dry the surface quickly and reduce the source, because condensation can be a sign of high humidity. EPA's general range is below 60 percent RH, ideally 30 to 50, though in a hard freeze your windows may set a lower ceiling than that.

NRC adds that visible condensation is less troublesome than the hidden kind in walls and attics. The University of Minnesota publication notes that window condensation is frequently cited as the key visible sign of too much indoor humidity, and warns that concealed condensation inside walls and ceilings can happen at the same or lower humidity.
